The cooking for a Kashmiri wedding starts in the dark. Accounts of the wazas' night agree on the hour: around 3 am, with the sheep slaughtered only hours before ([JK Arts Foundation](https://www.jkartsfoundation.in/post/kashmiri-wazwan-celebration-of-food-culture-and-people); [Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)). In the open kitchen Kashmiris call the *vur*, the fire is fed with logs and birch branches, the meat for the meatballs is pounded with walnut-wood hammers on stone, and the copper degs sit "under an autumn moon" ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710)). One of those pots holds rogan josh. Mohammad Shafi Khan, a second-generation waza from the Ahad family in downtown Srinagar, puts about twenty pieces of mutton into each utensil of it ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)). Hours later it will reach the trami as a deep red gravy with its fat risen to the top, four pieces to a platter ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710)).

Rogan josh is the Kashmiri dish most of the world has heard of, and the one most of the world gets wrong. Outside the Valley its name sits on tomato-red curries that no Kashmiri kitchen would recognise. Inside it, two communities cook two different dishes under the same name. And the name itself has at least three explanations. This is the story of all of that: where the dish came from, what it is called and why, what makes it red, who cooks it and how, and where it sits in the feast. For the dish itself, the recipe and where to eat it, see [rogan josh](/dishes/rogan-josh); this page is the long version of its history.

## What rogan josh is

Rogan josh is mutton or goat, usually on the bone, braised in a red gravy. In a Wazwan it is one of the seven dishes Wikipedia lists as "a must for these occasions", alongside tabak maaz, rista, daniwal korma, aab gosht, marchwangan korma and gushtaba ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan)); the Waza family's own cookbook names the same seven as "an indispensable part of the feast" ([Roli Books](https://rolibooks.com/product/wazwaan-traditional-kashmiri-cuisine/)), and a 2014 essay in *Dawn* counts it "among the seven permanent dishes in wazwan" ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/1149780)). A 2016 review in the *Journal of Ethnic Foods* lists it among the key meat products of the feast, with kabab, tabak maaz, aab gosh, yakhni, rista and goshtaba ([Rather, Masoodi and Akhter](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jef.2016.12.002)).

Its red comes from Kashmiri chilli and from a flower or a root, never from tomato ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)). It is eaten with rice: "with rice, not with a roti or a naan", as one Kashmiri hotelier put it ([Vir Sanghvi](https://virsanghvi.com/Article-Details.aspx?key=1076)).

## The name: ghee, heat, or red?

Nobody agrees on what "rogan josh" means. The dish is also written roghan josh and roghan ghosht, and the *Diner's Dictionary* says it is unclear which name is the original ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogan_josh)).

The Persian reading is the most common. *Roghan* is ghee or oil, and *jōš* is to stew or braise, so the name would mean "stewed in ghee" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogan_josh), citing Lizzie Collingham and the *Oxford Dictionary of Foreign Words and Phrases*). The food historian Lizzie Collingham writes that the Persian name "implies a stew of meat cooked in butter" ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/1149780)). A news-agency feature glosses it more vividly: "Rogan means fat and Josh means to cook on high heat" ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)).

The Kashmiri reading points at the colour instead. Pat Chapman's *India: Food and Cooking* takes *roghan* as "red", which with *gosht*, meat, gives "red meat", or with a word for juice gives "red juice" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogan_josh)). *Dawn* reports that "some food historians" link the name to the colour for the same reason ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/1149780)).

The dictionary of the Kashmiri language itself has the word, and it sides with the fat. Grierson's *Dictionary of the Kashmiri Language* glosses *rōgan* as "grease, varnish, polish; clarified butter, ghī" ([Grierson, p. 828](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=rogan&searchhws=no)). The *Diner's Dictionary* concludes that the exact etymology "remains uncertain" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogan_josh)), and that is where the evidence leaves it. What can be said is that the name, like those of yakhni, aab gosht, rista and gushtaba, comes from Persian ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine), citing the 2016 review).

Whichever reading is right, both describe the finished dish. Kashmiri cooks judge rogan josh by its fat: a Kashmiri cookbook first published in 1988 has it simmered "until the meat is tender and the gravy is red, thick, and oily" ([Kaul Kilam, *Culinary Art of Kashmir*](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/40.html)), and a magazine glossary describes it as "mutton slow cooked in ghee and its own juices" ([Outlook Traveller](https://www.outlookindia.com/traveller/ot-getaway-guides/kashmirs-legendary-wazwan-cuisine)). Red, and glossed with oil: the name fits either way.

## Where it came from: what's documented and what's told

### The Mughal story

The account most often repeated comes from Collingham's *Curry: A Tale of Cooks and Conquerors*. In it, rogan josh began in Persia as a dish of meat cooked in butter, came to North India with the Mughals, and was changed in Kashmir, where the Mughal court went to escape the summer heat of the plains and where cooks adapted Kashmiri herbs for colour and flavour. From there it became a staple of Kashmiri cooking and a main dish of the Wazwan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogan_josh)). Quoted directly, Collingham says "Rogan Josh originated in Persia", that the Mughals' presence "encouraged a blossoming of Kashmiri cuisine", and that the dish "was perfected" in Kashmir; the Mughals, she adds, cultivated Persian flavourings such as saffron and asafoetida so their cooks had a steady supply ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/1149780)). Kashmir came under Mughal rule in 1586 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_conquest_of_Kashmir)).

The food writer Vir Sanghvi tells the same story with the hedges left in. Rogan josh "came to India with the Mughals (we think) perhaps from Persia or some other part of the Middle East"; it was "cooked at Mughal courts everywhere" but "really found favour only in Kashmir"; and "it is the Kashmiris who created the version we know today" ([Vir Sanghvi](https://virsanghvi.com/Article-Details.aspx?key=1076)).

### An earlier Persian route

A second account places the Persian lesson two centuries before the Mughals. Shams-ud-din Ahmad, a former head of the Persian department at Kashmir University, wrote in his book *Shah-i-Hamdan aur Kashmir* that Persian chefs taught Kashmiris a set of mutton dishes: kabab, roganjosh, korma, yakhni, pulao, aabgosh, kofta and rista. The context is the arrival of saints from Iran and Central Asia from the 14th century, among them the saint the article names as Sayeed Ameer Kabir Hamdani, the Shah-i-Hamadan of the book's title ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338), which summarises the book). Mohammad Ashraf Wani, a former history professor at the same university, puts it this way: "Islam was not introduced in Kashmir just as a religion but more like a culture", the saints brought food, clothing and traditions with them, "that is when many dishes of wazwan were introduced", and the dishes were then changed until, in his words, "we won't find dishes with equal taste even in Iran and Central Asia" ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)).

### What the record holds

Neither account rests on a dated Kashmiri document that names the dish. No document records when or how Wazwan began; the accounts that circulate rest on word of mouth ([JK Arts Foundation](https://www.jkartsfoundation.in/post/kashmiri-wazwan-celebration-of-food-culture-and-people)). The nearest thing to early evidence is a Persian work completed in the early 17th century, the *Tuhfat-ul-Ahbab*, which describes 16th-century feasts with kabab, pulao and yakhni; rogan josh is not among the dishes *Kashmir Life* names in its summary ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/kashmir-wazwan-issue-42-vol-12-257951/)). Going further back, a Kashmir University historian, Professor Fayaz, told the same magazine that nothing like Wazwan appears in the *Nilmat Puran* or the *Rajatarangini*, and that experts who went looking for these dishes in Central Asia "could not find these there" ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/inalienable-ingredient-1499/)). The wazas' own tradition, that their craft came with cooks from Samarkand in Timur's time, runs into the fact that Timur never entered Kashmir; our [history of Wazwan](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/history-of-wazwan) sets out what is known.

So the firm ground is narrow: a Persian name, a dish that Kashmir made its own, and a feast whose documentation, in the words of the 2016 review, is "poorly documented" ([Rather, Masoodi and Akhter](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jef.2016.12.002)). The Mughal summer and the Hamadani-era cooks are both plausible, both told by serious people, and neither is proven.

## Why it is red

Rogan josh is red for three reasons, and the mix depends on who is cooking.

**Kashmiri chilli.** The Kashmiri red chilli is grown for colour rather than heat. Wikipedia puts it at 1,000 to 2,000 Scoville heat units, low enough to use by the handful ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_red_chilli)). In Kashmir the whole dried chillies are de-seeded, soaked and ground to a paste ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogan_josh)). Grierson's dictionary records the Kashmiri word for the chilli, *marʦ*, and a compound for "a chain or string of red chillis, dried and kept for winter use" ([Grierson, p. 594](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=red%20pepper&searchhws=no)): the strings of chillies drying on Srinagar's houses in autumn are the rogan josh of the coming winter.

**Mawal.** The second colouring is a flower. Mawal is the dried cockscomb flower, boiled to make a red colouring that is used mostly in Wazwan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)). Azmat Ali Mir, who runs a Kashmiri restaurant in Bangalore, credits it with the deep red of Wazwan dishes ([Asia Media Centre](https://www.asiamediacentre.org.nz/features/the-intricacies-of-kashmiri-wazwan)). Collingham names "the dried flower of the cockscomb plant (maval)" as the colouring of the Kashmiri Muslim version ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/1149780)); the cookbook writer Camellia Panjabi adds that some cooks use praan, the local shallot, and mawal petals for colour and for a supposed "cooling" effect ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogan_josh)). The waza Shafi Khan's rogan josh gets its dark red from red chillies and saffron ground in water together with cockscomb extract ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)).

**Ratan jot.** The third is a root. Ratan jot is alkanet, *Alkanna tinctoria*, whose dried flowers or root give a deep red ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogan_josh)). Its dye dissolves in oil but not in water, which is why it is fried in hot oil first ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkanna_tinctoria)). Wikipedia's article on Kashmiri cuisine associates it with Kashmiri Pandit cooking ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)), though not every Pandit account uses it: the hoteliers Vir Sanghvi consulted said Pandit cooks "will depend on Kashmiri red chillies and the browning of the yoghurt" for colour ([Vir Sanghvi](https://virsanghvi.com/Article-Details.aspx?key=1076)), and the 1988 cookbook's rogan josh gets its red from chilli powder and turmeric alone ([Kaul Kilam](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/40.html)).

Saffron plays a part too. A chef's notes from Kashmir have rogan josh and rista "further enhanced in colour by saffron extract" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine), citing Chef Jolly). And one Srinagar master chef was candid about what happens when the flowers run short: Bashir Ahmad Khan of Fateh Kadal told *Kashmir Life* he is "not averse to using food grade colors" so long as the other ingredients are unadulterated ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/inalienable-ingredient-1499/)).

What rogan josh is never red with is tomato. Wikipedia calls tomato an incorrect substitute, used when mawal and ratan jot can't be found, and states flatly that "no authentic Kashmir-cooked version uses tomatoes" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)). Tomatoes only entered Indian cooking after Europeans introduced them ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomato)); the dish is older than its British imitation, which is where the tomatoes come from, as the last section explains.

## The two rogan joshes

Kashmir has two kitchens, Kashmiri Muslim and Kashmiri Pandit, and both make rogan josh. The difference is the aromatics.

**The Wazwan version.** The base of the Kashmiri Muslim dish is praan, a shallot grown in Kashmir, with garlic. Sahapedia's account of the wazas' rogan josh has the meat simmered in a gravy of garlic, ginger, red chilli powder, clove, cardamom, cinnamon and praan paste ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)). "They are the life of Wazwan," Azmat Ali Mir says of praan; he adds that mustard oil is preferred for home cooking but that an authentic Wazwan can only be cooked in ghee ([Asia Media Centre](https://www.asiamediacentre.org.nz/features/the-intricacies-of-kashmiri-wazwan)). Reporters describe the wedding dish as shoulder meat cooked in praan paste, mawal flower essence, curd and Kashmiri chilli ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)), or "shoulder cuts cooked in flower essence, curd, and Kashmiri chilly" ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710)). Collingham sums it up as "lots of garlic and onion" with the cockscomb flower ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/1149780)).

**The Pandit version.** Kashmiri Pandit kitchens traditionally used no onion or garlic. J&K Tourism describes the Pandit school by "the abundant use of heeng (asafoetida) and curd", notes that "originally, Kashmiri Pandits avoided onions and garlics but now many of them have acquired a taste for them", and adds that almost no Pandit dish is cooked without curd ([JKTDC](https://www.jktdc.co.in/cuisines-of-kashmir.aspx)). M. Saleem Beg, the former director general of tourism, puts the contrast in one sentence: "Pandit cuisine uses dried ginger and asafoetida ... generously, while Muslim cookery uses onions and garlic" ([Daily Excelsior](https://www.dailyexcelsior.com/cuisines-of-kashmir-a-tradition-and-a-treasure-trove/)). Collingham agrees: Kashmiri Brahmans "avoid onions and garlic", so their rogan josh uses fennel seeds and asafoetida, and frying the asafoetida "released a garlicky flavour" that stands in for what is missing ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/1149780)). Vir Sanghvi's Kashmiri informants list the Pandit flavourings as *saunth* (dry ginger), aniseed, *hing* and dry spices, with garam masala sometimes going in at the end ([Vir Sanghvi](https://virsanghvi.com/Article-Details.aspx?key=1076)). A Pandit writer in *Outlook* describes a good rogan josh as cooked with little water and plenty of mustard oil ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/a-pandit-must-know-his-meat-magazine-192403)).

The published Pandit method bears this out. The 1988 *Culinary Art of Kashmir* makes its "Neni Rogan Josh" with mustard oil, curd, cloves, salt, a little sugar, red chilli powder, turmeric, dry ginger, aniseed, cumin, garam masala, a teaspoon of ghee and a pinch of asafoetida, and nothing from the onion family. The curd-coated meat is fried in mustard oil until golden brown; the chilli, turmeric and sugar go in with half a cup of water and are stirred until the meat is brownish-red; then water, dry ginger and aniseed, and a slow simmer until the gravy is "red, thick, and oily" ([Kaul Kilam](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/40.html)).

**The same dish, or two?** Sandeep Bamzai, a Kashmiri Pandit, has said the Muslim version is "completely different, not at all alike". Vir Sanghvi's verdict, having eaten both, is that they are "still recognisably the same dish" ([Vir Sanghvi](https://virsanghvi.com/Article-Details.aspx?key=1076)). Sources even disagree on the meat: J&K Tourism says Pandits prefer young goat ([JKTDC](https://www.jktdc.co.in/cuisines-of-kashmir.aspx)), while Wikipedia's article says lamb or sheep ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)). For the Pandit table more widely, see [Kashmiri Pandit food](/blog/kashmiri-pandit-food).

## The cut

Ask a waza what goes into rogan josh and the answer is a list of bones. Sahapedia describes pieces of about 70 to 80 g cut from the lower ribs, the lower back (*bukhtor*), the chest and the neck ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)). Shafi Khan's version uses medium chunks from the lower back ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)). Bashir Ahmad Choncha, who runs Choncha Foods in Srinagar, told *Deccan Chronicle* that "for Rogan josh neck and shoulders of the meat are preferable" ([Deccan Chronicle](https://www.deccanchronicle.com/lifestyle/food-and-recipes/020616/wazwan-from-the-valley.html)). Two wedding reports say shoulder ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html); [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710)), and a 2011 account of the trade has rogan josh and korma made from the bony portions of the lamb, with the back leg kept for gushtaba and kebabs and the ribs for tabak maaz ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/inalienable-ingredient-1499/)).

The common thread is meat on the bone from the back, ribs, shoulder and neck, not boneless cubes. Beyond that, the cut has always been a matter of means. Deepak Haksar of ITC Hotels, from a Kashmiri family, told Vir Sanghvi that "more prosperous families used better cuts of meat", naming "the whole machhli, the most tender part of the shoulder"; another Srinagar-born hotelier, Virender Razdan, said he never saw the lamb-shank version now sold in expensive restaurants as "Kareli Rogan Josh" while growing up in the Valley. Sanghvi's conclusion: "perhaps the cut you use is only a function of what you can afford", and what makes the dish is the spicing ([Vir Sanghvi](https://virsanghvi.com/Article-Details.aspx?key=1076)).

Freshness matters as much as the cut. At a wedding the meat is prepared for cooking within an hour of butchering ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)); the sheep are slaughtered only hours before the fires are lit ([JK Arts Foundation](https://www.jkartsfoundation.in/post/kashmiri-wazwan-celebration-of-food-culture-and-people)).

## How the wazas cook it

Rogan josh at a wedding is cooked by professionals. A Wazwan is made by a team of wazas under a head cook, the vasta waza, who may oversee 15 to 20 degs at once while apprentices chop, portion and pound ([Asia Media Centre](https://www.asiamediacentre.org.nz/features/the-intricacies-of-kashmiri-wazwan)). They work in a temporary kitchen set up at the host's home ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)), and they talk to each other, one account says, in a code of their own ([JK Arts Foundation](https://www.jkartsfoundation.in/post/kashmiri-wazwan-celebration-of-food-culture-and-people)). Our guide to [the wazas](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/waza-meaning) covers the trade; what follows is the part that bears on this dish.

**The pots.** The degs are copper, nickel-plated on the inside ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258); [Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)). One chef, Nazir Ahmed Aram, says the pots have to have the right copper content ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)). At one working-class wedding in Srinagar a reporter counted the vessels as "enormous brass vats" on a trench of burning logs, their heat regulated by two men lifting a vat on or off the fire ([Outlook Traveller](https://www.outlookindia.com/traveller/ot-getaway-guides/kashmirs-legendary-wazwan-cuisine)).

**The fire.** The wood is from fruit trees: apple, walnut or plum, with acacia and willow used occasionally ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)). The reasoning given for slow wood or charcoal heat is that the food "receives heat on two sides, top and bottom" and that "the best results are obtained by slow heat using charcoal" ([Daily Excelsior](https://www.dailyexcelsior.com/cuisines-of-kashmir-a-tradition-and-a-treasure-trove/)). Reuters' photographs from 2026 show the dishes cooked in *degchis* over a wood fire, and the agency credits the fire with the feast's smoky flavour ([Reuters via Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/gallery/2026/9/30/photos-kashmirs-600-year-old-wazwan-wedding-feast-adapts-to-modern-life)).

**The hours.** After a night of chopping and pounding, the cooking proper begins at dawn and takes about six hours ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)); in the wedding season a waza's day can run to 18 or 20 hours ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)). Bashir Ahmad Khan's rule for all of it: "In wazwan hurry does not achieve the best curry" ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/inalienable-ingredient-1499/)).

**The gravy.** The wazas' rogan josh, as Sahapedia records it, is the meat simmered in garlic, ginger, red chilli powder, clove, cardamom, cinnamon and praan paste ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)). Panjabi's account of the dish adds bay leaves and notes that it is often finished with *dampokhtak*, the slow, sealed cooking of the Mughal kitchen ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogan_josh)). The wedding version is "slow cooked in ghee and its own juices" ([Outlook Traveller](https://www.outlookindia.com/traveller/ot-getaway-guides/kashmirs-legendary-wazwan-cuisine)). It is done when the fat has risen: the *rogan* of the name, whichever way you read it.

**The serving.** The wazas themselves carry the pots to the tramis and serve "exactly four pieces of a course" onto the four quarters of each platter, one for each diner ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710)). The rice underneath, in the best feasts, is mushk budji, a short-grained aromatic variety grown in the higher reaches of the Valley ([Asia Media Centre](https://www.asiamediacentre.org.nz/features/the-intricacies-of-kashmiri-wazwan)) that received a geographical indication in February 2022 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)).

For a home version with quantities, see the recipe on [the rogan josh page](/dishes/rogan-josh).

## Its place at the trami

Rogan josh comes early in the gravies. Outlook India's account of the order runs: seekh kababs, tabak maaz, a large piece of mutton and chicken on the trami first; then methi maaz, rista, rogan josh, daniwal korma and marchwangan korma; then aab gosht, yakhni and, last, gushtaba ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258)). Asia Media Centre's has rista, then rogan josh, then daniphol and aab gosht ([Asia Media Centre](https://www.asiamediacentre.org.nz/features/the-intricacies-of-kashmiri-wazwan)). At the wedding Gulf News describes, rista came as the second course and rogan josh in the run of dishes that followed, between daniwal korma and marchwangan korma ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710)).

How fixed is the order? Within a kitchen, very: "when it comes to serving the various dishes, the order is set in stone", says Azmat Ali Mir ([Asia Media Centre](https://www.asiamediacentre.org.nz/features/the-intricacies-of-kashmiri-wazwan)). Between kitchens, less so: Outlook notes there are no set rules about which dishes are served ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258)). What every account shares is that the heavy red dishes come in the middle, with a radish chutney served between them to help digestion ([JK Arts Foundation](https://www.jkartsfoundation.in/post/kashmiri-wazwan-celebration-of-food-culture-and-people); [Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)), and that gushtaba closes the meal. [How Wazwan is served](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/how-wazwan-is-served) covers the trami, the hand-washing and the manners; [Traditional Wazwan](/dishes) lists every dish in serving order.

The scale is worth knowing before you sit down. A news-agency feature estimates "easily 2 kg of mutton and chicken" served per person across a full wedding Wazwan ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)). Rogan josh is one course of many.

## Rogan josh and its neighbours

Three other red or near-red dishes share the trami, and visitors confuse them.

- **Marchwangan korma** is the hot one. It is made, a waza says, with "smaller chunks of meat than used in roganjosh and cooked in a thick chilli gravy" ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)); by one estimate "three-quarters of the chilly used in the entire wazwan is singularly used in this dish" ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)). Rogan josh is the milder, more aromatic of the two. [Marchwangan korma](/dishes/marchwangan-korma) has its own page.
- **Rista** shares rogan josh's colourings, chilli and mawal or alkanet, but is pounded meat shaped into balls rather than pieces on the bone ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan)). See [rista](/dishes/rista).
- **Yakhni** is the white opposite: a yogurt gravy without turmeric or chilli powder ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Kashmiri_dishes)). **Daniwal korma** is paler still, "a white gravy flavoured with coriander" ([Outlook Traveller](https://www.outlookindia.com/traveller/ot-getaway-guides/kashmirs-legendary-wazwan-cuisine)). See [yakhni](/dishes/yakhni) and [daniwal korma](/dishes/daniwal-korma).

For the wider confusion between Kashmiri and Mughlai cooking, see [Wazwan vs Mughlai](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/wazwan-vs-mughlai).

## The dish abroad: how rogan josh turned tomato-red

The rogan josh most of the world knows was made in Britain. Collingham traces the dish's path from a mildly spiced Persian stew to the herb-red Kashmiri dish and then to a British Indian restaurant dish made red with tomatoes and red peppers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogan_josh), citing Collingham and the BBC's Sheila Dillon). Those restaurants mattered beyond Britain: Collingham notes that many foreign visitors, Americans among them, had their first taste of Indian food in them ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogan_josh)). For such diners, rogan josh and the tomato version are the same thing.

The substitutions are well documented. Madhur Jaffrey's 1982 BBC book suggests "4 to 16 times as much paprika as cayenne" to copy the colour without Kashmiri chillies; Sanjeev Kapoor's restaurant version uses white and black cardamom, anise and bay leaves; Shivaji Rao Holkar's 1975 *Cooking of the Maharajas* describes a dish of meat and tomatoes; ready-made pour-over sauces are tomato-based; and Dharamjit Singh's *Indian Cookery* of 1973 already objected that tomatoes are not traditional ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogan_josh)). Vir Sanghvi is blunter: a "dubious variation" of the dish "has long been a staple of the Kwality-Volga kind of restaurant" in India, the English curry-house version "tastes nothing like the real thing", and "the Punjabi restaurant variation is based on the Hindu recipe" ([Vir Sanghvi](https://virsanghvi.com/Article-Details.aspx?key=1076)).

None of this is a reason to avoid the dish outside Kashmir. It is a reason to know which rogan josh you are eating.

## Eating it for the first time

Weddings are private, so most visitors meet rogan josh in a Srinagar restaurant, as a single dish or as part of a set trami. Our [guide to eating in Srinagar](/restaurants/best-wazwan-srinagar) lists places that serve it, and the [cost guide](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/cost-guide) explains prices.

What to expect: a deep red gravy with a layer of oil on top, pieces of mutton on the bone, and no sweetness from tomato. Eat it with rice. Its heat varies from kitchen to kitchen: the chilli itself is mild, but India's Consumer Education and Research Centre found two branded "Kashmiri chilli" powders at 48,000 and 60,000 Scoville units ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_red_chilli)), so ask if you are sensitive. [Is Kashmiri food spicy?](/blog/is-kashmiri-food-spicy) covers the rest of the menu. If a menu offers a Pandit-style rogan josh, you now know what the difference is.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does rogan josh mean?

It is uncertain. Read as Persian, *roghan* means ghee or oil and *josh* means to stew or braise, so "stewed in ghee". Another reading takes *roghan* as the Kashmiri word for "red", giving "red meat" or "red juice". The *Diner's Dictionary* says the exact etymology remains uncertain, and Grierson's dictionary of Kashmiri glosses *rōgan* as grease or clarified butter.

### Where did rogan josh come from?

Its name is Persian. The food historian Lizzie Collingham writes that it originated in Persia, came to North India with the Mughals and was perfected in Kashmir, where the Mughal court spent its summers. A Kashmir University Persian scholar's account has Persian cooks teaching it to Kashmiris two centuries earlier, with the saints who came from Iran from the 14th century. No dated Kashmiri document names the dish, so neither account is proven.

### Why is rogan josh red?

From Kashmiri chilli, which colours strongly but is mild, and from mawal, the dried cockscomb flower, in the Kashmiri Muslim version, or ratan jot, the root of alkanet, in some Kashmiri Pandit versions. Saffron is sometimes added for colour. Authentic rogan josh cooked in Kashmir contains no tomato.

### Does rogan josh have tomatoes in it?

Not in Kashmir. Tomatoes came into the dish in British Indian restaurants, which used tomatoes and red peppers for the colour; tomato-based pour-over sauces followed. Wikipedia calls tomato an incorrect substitute used when mawal and ratan jot can't be found.

### Is rogan josh spicy?

Usually medium. Kashmiri chilli rates about 1,000 to 2,000 Scoville units, so the dish is red rather than hot, but some branded "Kashmiri chilli" powders have tested far hotter, and kitchens vary. The hot dish on the trami is marchwangan korma, not rogan josh.

### Which cut of meat is used for rogan josh?

Meat on the bone from the lower ribs, lower back, chest, neck and shoulder, in pieces of about 70 to 80 g, according to wazas and Srinagar chefs. Boneless cubes and lamb shanks are restaurant inventions.

### What is the difference between Kashmiri Muslim and Kashmiri Pandit rogan josh?

The Muslim, or Wazwan, version is built on praan (Kashmiri shallots) and garlic, coloured with mawal, and cooked in ghee at a feast. The Pandit version traditionally has no onion or garlic and relies on asafoetida, curd, dry ginger and fennel, with mustard oil. They are different dishes under one name, though many consider them recognisably the same.

### When is rogan josh served at a Wazwan?

Early among the gravies: after the first dishes on the trami and after rista, before the korma dishes, aab gosht, yakhni and the closing gushtaba. The order is fixed within a kitchen but varies between them.
