Between the two red dishes of a Wazwan comes a green one. After rogan josh and before marchwangan korma, in Outlook India's account of the order, the wazas serve daniwal korma, "mutton cooked in a lot of coriander" ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258)). A magazine glossary describes what arrives: "superbly tender meat on the bone, in a white gravy flavoured with coriander" ([Outlook Traveller](https://www.outlookindia.com/traveller/ot-getaway-guides/kashmirs-legendary-wazwan-cuisine)). It is the least written-about of the seven essential dishes, and the one whose name tells you most about it.

This is the story of a dish with a long name: what daniwal and korma each mean, where the herb and the word came from, why the herb is a spring plant at an autumn feast, what the dish is built on, what the sources agree and disagree on, how it is made, why it is not the korma the rest of the world knows, and where it sits in the meal. For what the dish is, with a recipe and where to eat it, see [daniwal korma](/dishes/daniwal-korma).

## What daniwal korma is

Daniwal korma is mutton braised in a yogurt gravy and finished with fresh coriander. Wikipedia's article on the dish calls it lamb or mutton "cooked in a yogurt-based gravy flavored with fresh coriander leaves" and "a staple of the Kashmiri Wazwan" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniwal_korme)), and its Wazwan article lists it among the seven dishes that are "a must" at the feast ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan)). The Waza family's cookbook names "dhaniwal korma" among the same seven ([Roli Books](https://rolibooks.com/product/wazwaan-traditional-kashmiri-cuisine/)), and the former tourism director M. Saleem Beg has "dhania kurma" in his list of seven standard dishes ([Daily Excelsior](https://www.dailyexcelsior.com/cuisines-of-kashmir-a-tradition-and-a-treasure-trove/)). Reporters at weddings describe "mutton cooked in coriander" ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710)) and "mutton korma cooked in coriander and ghee with yogurt-based gravy" ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)).

## The name: coriander, and a braise

Both halves of the name are in the dictionaries.

**Daniwal** is coriander, and specifically the leaf. Grierson's *Dictionary of the Kashmiri Language* enters *dāñĕwal* as the greens of the coriander plant, and distinguishes it from *dāñĕwal-gūjü*, the powdered and sifted seed, and *dāñĕwal-kulu*, the shrub itself ([Grierson, p. 232](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=coriander&searchhws=no)). The dish is named for the fresh herb, not the spice. A news feature cited by Wikipedia makes the same point in Hindi-Urdu terms: daniwal means coriander ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniwal_korme), citing Times Now). Hindi and Urdu use one word, dhania, for both leaf and seed; Kashmiri, on the dictionary's evidence, used a suffix to tell them apart, and the dish takes the bare word, the leaf.

**Korma** is a braise. Wikipedia's article on the dish glosses "korme" or "korma" as braise, citing Merriam-Webster ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniwal_korme)), and its article on korma says that "the English name is an anglicisation of the Hindi-Urdu qormā", which in turn derives from "the Turkic qawirma, '[a] fried thing'", though "korma and modern Turkish kavurma are quite different dishes" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korma)). The 2016 review of Wazwan's meat products says korma "has Turkish roots" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)), and when a former head of Kashmir University's Persian department listed the dishes that Persian cooks taught Kashmiris, korma was among them ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338), summarising Shams-ud-din Ahmad's *Shah-i-Hamdan aur Kashmir*). A search of Grierson's dictionary finds no entry for the word; it came into Kashmiri from outside, like the dish.

Daniwal korma, then, is coriander-braise: the name is a recipe.

## The herb

Coriander is one of the oldest cultivated flavourings in the world, and almost none of its history passes through Kashmir. The plant is native to the Mediterranean basin; seeds found at two sites in Israel may be "the oldest find of coriander in the world"; about half a litre of its fruits were recovered from the tomb of Tutankhamun, which suggests it was cultivated in Egypt, "as coriander does not grow wild in Egypt"; the Ebers Papyrus of about 1550 BCE lists its uses; a Linear B tablet from Pylos records it "cultivated for the manufacture of perfumes" in Mycenaean Greece; and the Torah uses it as a point of reference for the taste of manna ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriander)). The English name comes through Old French from the Latin *coriandrum*, and the Greek *koríandron* behind that may be related to *kóris*, the bed bug, the name given "on account of its fetid, bug-like smell" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriander)). The Greeks were describing the green plant. Most people, Wikipedia says, "perceive the leaves as having a fresh, slightly citrus taste", from the aldehydes 2-decenal and 2-dodecenal; the dried seed tastes of linalool and pinene instead, lemony and warm; and a minority of people, because of a variation in an olfactory receptor gene, OR6A2, taste the leaf as soap ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriander)). That is the only dish on the trami whose main flavour a share of the guests are born unable to enjoy.

In Indian cooking the two halves of the plant lead different lives. The seeds go into garam masala and the spice mixtures; the leaves are a garnish and a chutney herb, and, Wikipedia notes, "in Indian and Central Asian recipes, coriander leaves are used in large amounts and cooked until the flavor diminishes" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriander)). Daniwal korma does both at once. It cooks the leaf into the gravy in quantity, as the Central Asian method does, and most recipes, our own included, add a second handful fresh at the end, as a garnish would. The Outlook glossary's "white gravy flavoured with coriander" is the cooked herb; the green flecks on top are the raw one.

## A cool-season plant at an autumn feast

Coriander is a plant of cool weather and short days. "Both temperature and day length influence flowering and seed set," says Jim Myers, who holds the vegetable breeding chair at Oregon State University; in midsummer the plant "bolts into small white flowers and produces seed within four to six weeks", while in cooler, shorter days it grows leaves "for several weeks or months before flowering", which is why the university's extension service tells gardeners to sow in early spring and again in late summer, and to "sow seeds in succession" for a steady supply of leaf ([Oregon State University Extension](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/news/how-grow-cilantro-leaves-or-coriander-seeds)). Wikipedia's account of the Kashmiri year, drawn from *Kashmir Life*, places the herb accordingly: "Coriander (danival) is a cool herb whose season predominates between spring and summer in cooler areas" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)).

The feast, though, is an autumn one. The Srinagar doctors who studied a wedding in 2012 chose the season because it is "when most of marriages in Kashmir take place" ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi, SKIMS, 2012](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)); Reuters' 2026 report on the wazas opens as "the wedding season opens in autumn" ([Reuters via Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/gallery/2026/9/30/photos-kashmirs-600-year-old-wazwan-wedding-feast-adapts-to-modern-life)). Where the coriander for an October wedding comes from, whether a late-summer sowing in the Valley, a cooler side-valley, or the markets of the plains, none of our sources records. What they do record is that the Valley thought about its foods in terms of season and temperament. Walter Lawrence noted in 1895 that grains, fruits and vegetables were "classed as hot or cold and combined with care", and that dried vegetables and fruits were important in the winter ([Lawrence, p. 254](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)); *Kashmir Life*'s word for the herb, as Wikipedia relays it, is "cool". A green, cooling dish between the two chilli-red ones is a placement a Kashmiri cook would have had the vocabulary to explain; whether any waza explains it that way, we have not found written down.

One more thing the Valley's larder says about the herb is what it does not contain. Kashmiris dry their summer vegetables for winter: "during summer, vegetables are peeled, chopped, salted and sun-dried to preserve them for winter", and the stock of *hokh syun* includes dried tomatoes, dried fenugreek leaves, dried lotus stem, "dried shallot leaves (praan)", dried mint, dried bottle gourd, dried turnip, dried quince, dried collard greens and dried spinach ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)). Fenugreek and mint are dried; coriander is not on the list, in that account or any other we have read. The herb that names this dish is, on the evidence, eaten green or not at all, which is one more reason the dish reads as a seasonal one even when it is served in October.

Lawrence himself never mentions the herb. A full-text search of *The Valley of Kashmir* finds no "coriander" and no "dhania", and for that matter no "chillies"; his chapter on food names rice, vegetables, oil, salt and pepper, fowl, mutton and milk, and his chapter on crops is about grain, fruit and saffron ([Lawrence, p. 253](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). The settlement commissioner counted what paid revenue. The herbs of the kitchen garden were below his notice, which is one reason the dictionary, compiled by a philologist from the speech of ordinary people, is the better witness to what Kashmiris cooked with.

## The base: praan and the stock

Every account of the dish names coriander and yogurt. What the wazas build those on is the same base that underlies most of the feast: a stock, and the Valley's shallot.

"Much of Kashmiri cooking relies on a fragrant meat stock", and, in a sentence Wikipedia takes from a Kashmiri cookbook, "the main skill of a wazwan lies in the preparation of this stock made of onions and shallots" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)). The shallot is praan. "Shallots, known as praan, stand out, lending its unique flavour to a multitude of Kashmiri dishes, from soups and stews, to curries and kebabs"; "many of Mughal Emperor Akbar's feasts in the 16th century featured shallots"; and one village in Bandipora, Buthoo, "has earned renown for producing prized organic shallots" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine), citing Kashmir News Observer). A Kashmiri restaurateur in Bangalore calls praan "the life of Wazwan" ([Asia Media Centre](https://www.asiamediacentre.org.nz/features/the-intricacies-of-kashmiri-wazwan)). Grierson's dictionary has no headword for shallot, but it has *prān*, "fried onions (as a seasoning)", with compounds for cooking a dish with them, and *ganḍ*, the ordinary onion bulb ([Grierson, pp. 761 and 289](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=onion&searchhws=no)). The dictionary's definition is a method as much as a word: praan is the onion in its fried, seasoning form, and that is how it goes into the korma.

How the praan is fried decides the dish, and the rule is stated in the same Wikipedia account, drawn from a Guardian recipe: "whole spices must be tempered, fried in oil – clove, cardamom, cinnamon, bay leaves. Fried too little, and the dish will be lifeless, without fragrance. A second too much, and one will be left with nothing but bitterness"; and at the end, "hot ghee or mustard oil is poured on top and garnished with fried praan (shallots) paste & saffron extract" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)). Chef Jolly's "lightly roasted" until golden is that rule in a waza's hands. The dictionary's word for the result, *bagāra*, is oily or buttery cooked food, and the Hindustani cook's word for the technique, bagar, the tempering of spices in hot fat, is the one Wikipedia's korma article uses for the korma's finish ([Grierson, p. 92](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=butter&searchhws=no); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korma)). The shallot leaves, too, are dried for winter as *praan* among the Valley's *hokh syun* ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)); the bulb goes into the korma, the leaf into the winter.

The 1988 Pandit cookbook from which Wikipedia takes its stock sentence belongs to the kitchen that does not use onions at all; the Pandit table replaces praan and garlic with asafoetida and dry ginger, a difference every source on the two cuisines records ([Daily Excelsior](https://www.dailyexcelsior.com/cuisines-of-kashmir-a-tradition-and-a-treasure-trove/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)). Daniwal korma, with its praan and garlic roasted golden, is a Wazwan dish in the Muslim manner; we have found no Pandit version by that name. The online index of that 1988 cookbook, which has sections for the mutton dishes of Kashmiri Pandits and a separate one for the cuisine of the Valley's Muslims, lists no korma of any kind and no coriander dish ([Kaul Kilam, *Culinary Art of Kashmir*, index](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/index2.html)). [Kashmiri Pandit food](/blog/kashmiri-pandit-food) covers the other table.

## The seed that stays out of the pot

The dish is named for the leaf, and the spice box of the Wazwan explains why it had to be. Coriander seed, which the plains grind into every garam masala, is almost absent from the feast's recipes. Sahapedia's rogan josh is seasoned with garlic, ginger, red chilli, clove, cardamom, cinnamon and praan; its aab gosht with shallot, garlic, ginger powder, fennel powder, cloves, cardamoms and cinnamon; its gushtaba gravy with shallots, ginger, garlic, cinnamon, cardamom, cumin and dry mint ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)). The Waza family's tabak maaz uses turmeric, dry ginger, cloves and black cardamom ([Scroll, from *Wazwaan*](https://scroll.in/food/945552/tabakh-maaz)); the 1988 cookbook's rogan josh uses chilli, turmeric, dry ginger, aniseed, cumin, garam masala and asafoetida ([Kaul Kilam](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/40.html)). The Valley's foundation spices are fennel and dry ginger, *bödiyāna* and *shō̃ṭh* in the dictionary ([Grierson, pp. 89 and 899](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=fennel&searchhws=no)), and our guide to [fennel and dry ginger](/blog/fennel-and-dry-ginger-wazwan) sets out how they do the work that coriander seed and cumin do elsewhere.

So when the Wazwan wanted coriander, it did not reach for the ground seed it hardly used; it reached for the fresh plant, in quantity, and gave it a dish of its own. Grierson's distinction between *dāñĕwal*, the greens, and *dāñĕwal-gūjü*, the sifted powder, is the distinction the feast draws in practice. That is our reading of the recipes; no waza is on record explaining the choice.

## What the sources agree on, and don't

Every account has the coriander and the yogurt. They part on the rest.

Wikipedia disagrees with itself. The body of its Wazwan article describes "lamb roasted with yoghurt, spices and onion puree, topped with coriander leaves"; the same article's list of dishes calls it "a mutton curry with coriander", and its list of Kashmiri dishes has "lamb cooked with coriander or parsley" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Kashmiri_dishes)). None of those three lines carries a citation, and "roasted" and "parsley" appear nowhere else. Every reported feast and every recipe has a braise, and coriander.

Colour is barely described. Outlook Traveller's "white gravy" is the only account that gives it one ([Outlook Traveller](https://www.outlookindia.com/traveller/ot-getaway-guides/kashmirs-legendary-wazwan-cuisine)); none of the sources mentions chilli. That places daniwal korma with the pale dishes of the feast, yakhni and aab gosht, rather than with rista and rogan josh, which take their red from Kashmiri chilli and mawal.

## How it is made

The one step-by-step account we have comes from a chef's notes on cooking with a Gulmarg waza. The lamb is blanched and the scum removed; praan, the Kashmiri shallot, and garlic are "lightly roasted" until golden, and a little stock is added; the cooked lamb goes back in with fresh coriander and spices, and blanched almonds are optional ([Chef Jolly](https://www.chefjolly.com/culinary-expedition-to-kashmir/)). The yogurt that every other account names ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniwal_korme); [Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)) gives the gravy its body and its paleness. The meat is on the bone: in the wazas' division of a carcass, the bony pieces go to rogan josh and the kormas ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/inalienable-ingredient-1499/)). At the wedding Outlook Traveller watched, the head waza sat "harvesting choice boneless chunks from lamb legs for korma" ([Outlook Traveller](https://www.outlookindia.com/traveller/ot-getaway-guides/kashmirs-legendary-wazwan-cuisine)), so kitchens differ on the bone as on much else.

The method is a korma's in the general sense Wikipedia gives the word: meat "braised with yogurt, water or stock, and spices", after being "first cooked briskly, or seared, using high heat, traditionally with ghee" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korma)). The fat is the feast's. A Kashmiri restaurateur says ghee is used for an authentic Wazwan and mustard oil at home ([Asia Media Centre](https://www.asiamediacentre.org.nz/features/the-intricacies-of-kashmiri-wazwan)); IANS has the korma "cooked in coriander and ghee" ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)). That ghee was, a century ago, a thing the Valley sold rather than ate: Lawrence found that "clarified butter (ghi) is not much eaten" and that Kashmiris cooked with vegetable oil, while ghi was the state's most important export to the Punjab ([Lawrence, p. 254 and the chapter on exports](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). Our [tabak maaz story](/blog/tabak-maaz-ribs-cooked-twice) follows the ghee.

The coriander goes in at the end, which is why the dish tastes of the herb rather than of a spice. Our own [recipe](/dishes/daniwal-korma) adds it twice, once to cook into the gravy and once fresh.

A chicken version exists. The waza Mohammad Shafi Khan names "daniwal korma kokar", chicken prepared with coriander, among the dishes hosts have asked for and wazas have added to the feast ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)); Wikipedia notes, without a citation, that lamb and chicken kormas are both common in the Wazwan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korma)).

## Not that korma

Outside Kashmir, korma means something else, and the something else has a documented history that the Kashmiri dish does not share. "Korma stems from Mughlai cuisine"; "kormas were prepared in the Mughal court kitchens", where, according to the food historian Neha Vermani, the dish is first mentioned in cookery books from the reign of Shah Alam; and "during the 18th century, cooks in the Mughal court enriched the Persian-style stew with almonds, garlic, spices, and yoghurt" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korma)). In Hyderabad in 1832 a "korma" was a variety of pilau with thinly sliced meat. In the nineteenth century the dish split in two. A *quarama* from Lucknow "contained (among other ingredients) ghee, yoghurt, cream, crushed almonds, cloves, cardamom, and saffron; whereas an 1869 Anglo-Indian quorema or korma, 'different in substance as well as name', had no cream, almonds, or saffron, but added the then-standard British curry spices, namely coriander, ginger, and black peppercorns" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korma)). From there it went, by way of the restaurant trade, to Britain, where "a typical korma as served in curry houses is a mildly spiced dish with a thick sauce" that "often features almonds, cashews or other nuts, and coconut", and where chicken korma is regularly named among the country's most popular curries ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korma)). The pot "may be sealed with dough during the last stages of cooking", the dum, and the spices finished in hot ghee, the bagar ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korma)).

The only korma in that whole history to contain coriander is the Anglo-Indian one of 1869, and its coriander was the ground seed of the British curry powder. Kashmir's coriander korma is the opposite case: the fresh leaf, with no cream, no nuts and no curry powder. The two dishes share a word and a verb and nothing in the pot. That is our comparison; the sources describe each korma without mentioning the other.

The Kashmiri korma shares the word and the braising and little more. Its base is yogurt and stock, its aromatics praan and garlic, and its flavour a fresh herb; nothing in the accounts above is sweet, creamy or thick with nuts, the optional almonds aside, and nothing is sealed under dough. The one sentence in Wikipedia's korma article that mentions the Valley, "in the traditional wazwan, a banquet produced by Hindu and Muslim cooks for a range of celebrations, lamb and chicken kormas are common elements", carries no citation and gets the feast's cooks wrong; the wazas are a Muslim guild ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korma); [Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)). The two kormas of the seven, daniwal and marchwangan, are named for what flavours them, coriander and chilli, not for a court. For the wider difference between the two kitchens, see [Wazwan vs Mughlai](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/wazwan-vs-mughlai); for the other korma, see our [marchwangan korma story](/blog/marchwangan-korma-chilli).

## The course that can go missing

Daniwal korma is one of the seven, and it is the one most easily left out. When the Srinagar doctors listed the dishes served at the wedding they studied in 2012, "in the order in which they are served", there were eleven, and daniwal korma was not among them: methi, chicken, kabab, tabakmaaz, dani, rista, roganjosh, tomato paneer, aab gosh, spinach, marchwangan korma and goshtaba ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)). That is one wedding, and the authors note that the number of dishes depends on "the nature of occasion and financial strength of host". But it fits what the wazas say about the seven: they are a norm, not a law. A veteran waza told Reuters that "there were only seven mutton dishes at that time", and that his trade has added a dozen more since ([Reuters](https://kfgo.com/?p=1357989)); Outlook India reports "no set rules" on which dishes a host serves ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258)). It also means that the one weighed record of a Wazwan's calories has no figure for this dish, and that the 2026 health advisory, which names rista, gushtaba, kebabs and tabak maaz, does not name it either ([Kashmir Reader](https://kashmirreader.com/2026/06/19/choose-moderation-in-wazwan-as-single-trami-may-contain-6000-9000-calories-health-advisory/)). On the evidence of its ingredients, a yogurt braise of meat on the bone without the meatballs' pounded fat, it is unlikely to be the heaviest thing on the trami; but that is a guess, and we would rather say so than invent a number.

## Where it comes in the meal

Daniwal korma comes in the middle. Outlook India's order has it after rogan josh and before marchwangan korma ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258)); at the wedding Gulf News describes it arrived in the run of dishes between the rista and the yakhni, with rogan josh, marchwangan korma and the greens ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710)). A pale, herbal dish between two red ones: that is where the wazas put it. [How Wazwan is served](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/how-wazwan-is-served) gives the whole order.

## Eating it for the first time

Daniwal korma is the mild, fresh-tasting dish of the feast, and the easiest to like if red gravies are not for you. In a Srinagar restaurant it is served as a plate of mutton on the bone in a pale gravy flecked with green, eaten with rice; our [guide to eating in Srinagar](/restaurants/best-wazwan-srinagar) lists places, and the [cost guide](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/cost-guide) covers prices. If you dislike coriander, this is the one dish to pass on; if coriander tastes of soap to you, you now know why, and the wazas will not be offended.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is daniwal korma?

Mutton on the bone braised in a yogurt gravy with praan and garlic and finished with a great deal of fresh coriander. It is one of the seven essential dishes of a Wazwan and is served in the middle of the meal.

### What does daniwal mean?

Coriander leaf. Grierson's dictionary of Kashmiri enters *dāñĕwal* as the greens of the coriander plant, distinct from *dāñĕwal-gūjü*, the powdered seed.

### What does korma mean in Kashmir?

A braise: meat cooked with yogurt, water or stock. The word is an anglicisation of the Hindi-Urdu *qormā*, from the Turkic *qawirma*, "a fried thing", and the 2016 review of Wazwan's meat products says it has Turkish roots.

### What is praan?

The Kashmiri shallot, fried as a seasoning and pounded into a paste; a Kashmiri restaurateur calls it "the life of Wazwan". Grierson's dictionary defines *prān* as "fried onions (as a seasoning)". The village of Buthoo in Bandipora is known for it.

### Does daniwal korma use coriander seed?

No. It is named for and made with the fresh leaf. Coriander seed is almost absent from the Wazwan's recipes, whose foundation spices are fennel and dry ginger; the one korma in the wider history of the word that contained coriander seed was an Anglo-Indian curry-powder version of 1869.

### Is daniwal korma spicy?

No. None of the accounts we have read mentions chilli, and the one that describes the colour calls the gravy white. Its flavour is coriander, yogurt, shallot and garlic.

### Why does coriander taste of soap to some people?

Because of a variation in an olfactory receptor gene, OR6A2, which makes some people sensitive to the aldehydes in the leaf. For everyone else the leaf tastes fresh and faintly citrus.

### Is it the same as a Mughlai korma?

No. The Mughlai korma was enriched in the 18th-century court kitchens with almonds, garlic, spices and yoghurt, and the British curry-house korma is mild, thick and often made with nuts and coconut. The Kashmiri daniwal korma is yogurt and stock with praan, garlic and fresh coriander.

### How many calories are in daniwal korma?

No published figure exists. The one weighed study of a Wazwan, at a Srinagar wedding in 2012, recorded eleven dishes and daniwal korma was not served; the 2026 health advisory does not name it.

### When is daniwal korma served at a Wazwan?

In the middle of the meal: after rogan josh and before marchwangan korma in one account, in the run of dishes between rista and yakhni in another.

## Fact-check list (for editors; not for publication)

Every claim is listed with its source in `../research/06-daniwal-korma.md`. Points to weigh:

1. **The Mughlai comparison** now rests on Wikipedia's Korma article (Mughal court kitchens; 18th-century enrichment; curry-house korma), quoted with its citation status checked; the statement that nothing in the Kashmiri accounts is sweet, creamy or nut-thickened is checked against the sources in the ledger. The sentence calling Wikipedia's wazwan line wrong about "Hindu and Muslim cooks" rests on Sahapedia's and other accounts of the wazas as a Muslim hereditary trade; keep it measured.
2. **"The coriander goes in at the end"** is inferred from Chef Jolly's method (coriander added with the cooked lamb) and the name's reference to the fresh leaf; Chef Jolly is a personal blog, attributed as such.
3. **Wikipedia's "roasted" and "parsley"** lines are quoted as uncited and set against the reported feasts.
4. **"Chicken version"**: Khan's remark that it was added at hosts' demands is attributed. Wikipedia's "lamb and chicken kormas are common" is noted as uncited.
5. **The seasonal paragraph** says explicitly that no source records where autumn coriander comes from and that no waza is on record explaining the placement in hot-and-cold terms. Keep both disclaimers.
6. **Lawrence's silence** is a negative result from the Internet Archive's full-text search of the 1895 scan ("coriander", "dhania", "chillies", "capsicum" return nothing; "red pepper" once, in the chapter on Hindus). The article says so.
7. **The missing-course section** rests on one wedding's dish list (SKIMS 2012). The article says it is one wedding and declines to give a calorie figure.
8. The article makes no claim about colour beyond Outlook Traveller's "white gravy".
9. **Outlook Traveller's byline.** The page credits Benny Joshua, 28 July 2017; the earlier draft credited Rimli Sengupta, which does not appear on the page. Corrected.
10. **"The seed that stays out of the pot"** is an argument from the absence of coriander seed in the specific recipes cited (Sahapedia's three, the Roli tabak maaz, Kaul Kilam's rogan josh). It is flagged as our reading. An editor who knows a waza's recipe that uses coriander seed should soften it.
11. **The hokh syun list** is Wikipedia's (cited there to Moneycontrol and Outlook Traveller); the observation that coriander is not dried is ours and is stated as "in that account or any other we have read".
12. **The Guardian-sourced frying rule** is quoted from Wikipedia's Kashmiri cuisine article as Wikipedia gives it; the Guardian recipe itself was not read.
13. **Kaul Kilam's index** was read on 15 October 2026; "no korma of any kind and no coriander dish" is a statement about recipe titles in that index.
