Every Kashmiri house dries its chillies in autumn. Grierson's *Dictionary of the Kashmiri Language* has a word for the result: *marʦ-wã̄gan-āra*, "a chain or string of red chillis, dried and kept for winter use", with the note that "they are strung on a thread, like a necklace" ([Grierson, p. 594](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=red%20pepper&searchhws=no)). Those strings are the dish this story is about. Of all the chilli that goes into a wedding Wazwan, one estimate has three-quarters going into a single pot ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)). The pot is marchwangan korma, "a fiery hot mutton cooked with Kashmiri red chilli" ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258)), and it is the one dish on the trami that a Kashmiri will warn you about.

The warning needs qualifying, because the chilli in question is famous for being mild. This story covers the name, where the chilli came from and what the Valley called pepper before it arrived, what the chilli is and how hot the dish really gets, how heat is measured and why the measurements disagree, the application to protect the Valley's long chilli, how the dish differs from the rogan josh it sits beside, what it costs the body and what is served to cool it, and where it comes in the meal. For what the dish is, with a recipe and where to eat it, see [marchwangan korma](/dishes/marchwangan-korma).

## What marchwangan korma is

Marchwangan korma is mutton braised in a thick gravy built on Kashmiri red chilli. Wikipedia's list of Kashmiri dishes describes "meat cooked with spices and yogurt and mostly using Kashmiri red chillies and hot in taste", and its Wazwan article lists the dish among the seven that are "a must" at the feast ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Kashmiri_dishes); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan)). The Waza family's cookbook names "marchwagan korma" among the seven indispensable dishes ([Roli Books](https://rolibooks.com/product/wazwaan-traditional-kashmiri-cuisine/)), and the former tourism director M. Saleem Beg has "mirchi korma" in his seven ([Daily Excelsior](https://www.dailyexcelsior.com/cuisines-of-kashmir-a-tradition-and-a-treasure-trove/)). At weddings, reporters have called it "fiery hot Kashmiri chilly korma" ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710)) and "mutton in a gravy red with Kashmiri chillies, flavoured with cardamom and coriander" ([Outlook Traveller](https://www.outlookindia.com/traveller/ot-getaway-guides/kashmirs-legendary-wazwan-cuisine)). Doctors who recorded a Srinagar wedding feast in 2012 wrote it down as "pieces of meat prepared in chilly soup", four to six pieces to a trami ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi, SKIMS, 2012](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)).

Its spelling is the least settled of the seven. Marchwangan, marchhwangan, martsewagan, martswangan, marchwagun, marchwagan, marchawangan and mirchi korma all appear in the sources above; they are one dish.

## The name: a string of chillies, and a braise

The first word is the chilli. *Marʦ* is Grierson's word for it, and the dictionary's gloss shows how the word was used: "long pepper, red pepper, a pod of red pepper, a red chilli, Capsicum sp." His compound for the dried string, *marʦ-wã̄gan-āra*, carries the element *-wã̄gan-*, and the dictionary's separate entry *wã̄gun* is the brinjal, or egg-plant, dried and kept for winter in the same way ([Grierson, pp. 594 and 1102](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=brinjal&searchhws=no)). Marchwangan, in other words, is the Kashmiri word for the chilli pod itself, formed from the chilli and the plant word it shares with the brinjal. That is our reading of the two entries; the dictionary does not spell it out.

The second word is the braise. Korma is an anglicisation of the Hindi-Urdu *qormā*, from "the Turkic qawirma, '[a] fried thing'", for meat "braised with yogurt, water or stock, and spices" after being seared "using high heat, traditionally with ghee" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korma)), a word the 2016 review of Wazwan's meat products says "has Turkish roots" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)). Marchwangan korma is chilli-braise, as daniwal korma is coriander-braise: the two kormas of the seven are named for what flavours them. Our [daniwal korma story](/blog/daniwal-korma-coriander) covers the other one, and the word's own journey from the Mughal court to the British curry house.

## Pepper before chilli

The gloss on *marʦ* records something the dish's name has forgotten: the word is older than the pod. The first sense Grierson gives is "long pepper", *Piper longum*, the Indian spice that Europe bought before it bought chilli, and the dictionary has two more words for the same plant, *maga* and *pipijü*, and one, *loṭu*, for the cubeb, the "tail pepper" of the old trade ([Grierson, pp. 594, 551, 749 and 535](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=pepper&searchhws=no)). Pepper was in the Kashmiri kitchen's vocabulary and in its mortar: the dictionary's *nal* is a small stone mortar used for pounding pepper and other spices. It was in the sickroom, where *ʦhaṭh* was "a meat gravy seasoned only with a little salt and pepper, given to sick people", and in the shepherds' camp, where *ʦarŏn* was a relish of sheep's or goat's liver spiced with very hot pepper and salt, also offered to demons ([Grierson, pp. 632, 1063 and 1082](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=pepper&searchhws=no)). When the capsicum arrived, Kashmiri did what most languages did: it gave the new pod the old pepper's name. The Portuguese had done the same with Columbus, who "and his crew called the Capsicum fruits 'peppers'" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chili_pepper)). A dish named for the red strings on the house front is, at one remove, named for a spice that came from the opposite direction.

## Where the chilli came from

The chilli is the newest thing on the trami. "Capsicum plants originated in modern-day Peru and Bolivia, and have been a part of human diets since about 7,500 BC", and "chili peppers were cultivated in east-central Mexico some 6,000 years ago" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chili_pepper)). A physician on Columbus's second voyage, Diego Álvarez Chanca, published the first European account of them in 1494; the Portuguese then carried them "first to Africa and Arabia" and on to their Asian ports, "including Goa, Sri Lanka, and Malacca"; and "the earliest known mention of the chili pepper in Chinese writing dates to 1591", the plant having probably entered China in the 1570s ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chili_pepper); the Portuguese sentences are uncited on the page). A Malayalam news feature's summary of the Indian story is the usual one: the chilli came with the Portuguese in the sixteenth century, and before it India's heat was black pepper ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)).

When it reached Kashmir, nobody has recorded. The Valley's own chilli is the one the trade calls Kashmiri, grown, according to the Spices Board, "mainly in Jammu and Kashmir and Himachal Pradesh, but also in places such as Goa", and "harvested during winter" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_red_chilli)). The Goa connection is worth noticing: the port where the Portuguese landed the plant still grows a chilli by the Valley's name. Whether the long, mild, deep-red Kashmiri pod was selected in Kashmir from the Portuguese stock, or came up from the coast already mild, is not something any source we have read can say. What can be said is that the feast's "fiery" dish depends on an American plant that has been in Asia for five hundred years at most, and that the Valley's oldest dictionary records it already strung on the house fronts.

## Lawrence, 1895

The one detailed outsider's account of Kashmiri food before the twentieth century barely knows the chilli exists. A full-text search of Walter Lawrence's *The Valley of Kashmir* finds no "chillies", no "capsicum" and no "coriander"; his chapter on food lists rice and grains, vegetables, oil, "salt and pepper", fowl, mutton and milk, and records that Kashmiris liked their food very salty, that clarified butter was "not much eaten", and that the cultivator ate mutton "perhaps five times in the month" ([Lawrence, pp. 253 to 254](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). "Red pepper" appears once, in the chapter on the Hindus, and it is telling. The Pandits, Lawrence wrote, had once eaten only water-chestnut on fast days, but "vegetable marrows, and red pepper are now freely eaten", a laxity his informants regretted; and they avoided a whole class of foods for their colour: "Tomatoes, the red-fleshed kabuli vegetable marrow, carrots, and red beans are an abomination to the Hindu" ([Lawrence, p. 300](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). Red pepper, newly admitted on fast days, had passed the colour bar that tomatoes had not. That is all the 1895 record says about the pod. It says nothing about any dish made from it, which is one more reason to be wary of anyone who dates marchwangan korma.

Lawrence does record the larder the strings belonged to. Dried vegetables and fruits, he wrote, were important in the winter ([Lawrence, p. 254](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)); the dictionary's *marʦ-wã̄gan-āra* and its *wã̄gun*, the brinjal dried the same way, are two items from that larder. The larder is still kept. "During summer, vegetables are peeled, chopped, salted and sun-dried to preserve them for winter", and the stock of *hokh syun* runs to dried tomatoes, fenugreek leaves, lotus stem, shallot leaves, mint, bottle gourd, turnip, quince, collard greens and spinach ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)). The chilli strings on the house front are the red end of that store. A dish that uses three-quarters of a feast's chilli at an October wedding is a dish that draws on the summer's drying, and on the winter's.

## How hot is it?

Here the dish and its reputation part company, because the Kashmiri chilli is mild. Kashmiri red chillies "are characterized by their ability to give a dark red colour to food and add flavour, while not allowing the food to become too pungent or spicy"; the dried pod "appears wrinkled and rough, often brownish when traditionally dried (long dry), but contains densely concentrated red pigment material"; its ASTA colour value, the spice trade's measure of extractable colour, is 54.10; its capsaicin, the compound that makes a chilli hot, is 0.325 per cent, and a 1999 study found 0.126 per cent; and on the Scoville scale it reads 1,000 to 2,000 units ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_red_chilli)). Wikipedia's article on Kashmiri cuisine calls powdered red chillies "moderately hot colouring agents" used in most meat dishes, and records that Kashmiri Muslim cooking "uses chilies in moderate quantity, and avoid hot dishes at large meals" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)). A feast that avoids hot dishes, and one dish that is fiery: the contradiction resolves in the quantity. The chilli is mild; marchwangan korma uses a great deal of it. By the IANS estimate, three-quarters of all the chilli in a Wazwan goes into this dish alone ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)).

The scale on which the chilli is rated is older than most of the sources above and less exact than its numbers look. It is named for Wilbur Scoville, an American pharmacist whose 1912 "Scoville organoleptic test" dissolved an exact weight of dried pepper in alcohol, diluted it in sugar water, and gave the dilutions to a panel of five tasters until "at least three" could no longer detect any heat; the rating was that dilution, in multiples of 100 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scoville_scale)). "A weakness of the Scoville organoleptic test is its imprecision due to human subjectivity", and tasters' palates tire quickly; modern laboratories measure capsaicinoids by chromatography instead and convert the result to Scoville units, but results can "vary widely (up to ± 50%) between laboratories" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scoville_scale)). On that scale a bell pepper is 0, a jalapeño 2,500 to 10,000, a cayenne 25,000 to 50,000, pure capsaicin 16 million ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scoville_scale)). A Kashmiri chilli at 1,000 to 2,000 sits below the mildest jalapeño; what capsaicin it has works the way all capsaicin does, by binding "with pain receptors in the mouth and throat" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chili_pepper)).

Kashmiri has words for what capsaicin does. Grierson's *khōla* is the burning or stinging sensation caused by pepper and other pungent things, and *bĕgarun* describes food that has acquired an acrid, burning taste ([Grierson, pp. 398 and 92](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=pepper&searchhws=no)). The Valley's chilli, on the trade's measurements, delivers less of the first than almost any chilli in India, which is why it is wanted everywhere. Vir Sanghvi "writes that a majority of the restaurant industry in India uses Kashmiri chillies or their powdered form", and chefs use it as a stand-in for the Goan peri-peri; "India is the largest consumer and producer", and the big spice brands all sell a powder by its name ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_red_chilli)). A chilli grown in two hill states and wanted by every tandoor in the country is a chilli that will be imitated.

How hot the korma gets depends on the kitchen, and on the chilli. "Due to the high demand for Kashmiri chillis, substitutes such as Byadagi chillies are often used", and India's Consumer Education and Research Centre has tested two branded "Kashmiri chilli" powders at 48,000 and 60,000 Scoville units, with brick powder recorded as "one of the forms of adulteration" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_red_chilli)). A powder labelled Kashmiri is not always the mild one, and a korma made from it is a different dish. The Valley's own chilli is also sometimes "added in excess" purely for colour ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)). A chef who cooked the dish with a Gulmarg waza noted its "sharpness due to the chili extract" ([Chef Jolly](https://www.chefjolly.com/culinary-expedition-to-kashmir/)). A Srinagar master chef has said he is "not averse to using food grade colors" when the rest of the pot is honest ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/inalienable-ingredient-1499/)), which is a reminder that red and hot are not the same thing: the ASTA value measures the first and the Scoville number the second, and the Kashmiri chilli is high on one and low on the other. Our guide to [Kashmiri red chilli](/blog/kashmiri-red-chili) covers the spice, and [Is Kashmiri food spicy?](/blog/is-kashmiri-food-spicy) the rest of the menu.

## A GI for the long chilli

The imitators got their protection first. The Spices Board's register of geographical indications for spices, as of 15 September 2026, runs to 47 entries, and the chillies on it include Guntur Sannam (registered 2010) and Byadagi (2011), both with the Spices Board itself as custodian, Goa's Khola chilli (2019) and Harmal chilli (2021), Nagaland's Naga Mircha, Mizoram's Mizo chilli, Manipur's Hathei, Tamil Nadu's Ramnathapuram Mundu and Virudhunagar Samba Vattal, Uttarakhand's Almora Lakhori, and Madhya Pradesh's Khargone Lal Mirch, registered in March 2026. Jammu and Kashmir appears twice on the list, for Kashmir saffron, registered on 1 May 2020 with the Directorate of Agriculture at Lalmandi, Srinagar, as its proprietor, and for Ramban's dried pomegranate seed, registered in January 2024. No Kashmiri chilli appears on it ([Spices Board of India, list of spice GIs](https://www.indianspices.com/sites/default/files/List_of_GItags_for_spices_BJ.pdf)). The Byadagi that is sold in its place, and two chillies from the Goan coast where the Portuguese landed the plant, have the legal name that the Valley's own pod does not.

The Valley is trying to change that. There are "government initiatives and incentives in Jammu and Kashmir to support and increase the production of the local variety of the Kashmiri chilli" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_red_chilli)); on 4 June 2021 an adviser to the lieutenant governor, Farooq Ahmad Khan, visited Baramulla and, at a vegetable cluster at Sadderbal Palhalan in Pattan, launched "a special programme for revival of local Chilli known as 'Lal Mirchi'" ([Kashmir Reader](https://kashmirreader.com/?p=99101)). Three years later the application came from the same tehsil. On 26 June 2024 a farmers' company in Pattan, Baramulla, the Shejaar Vegetable Producer Company Limited, applied to India's Geographical Indications Registry to register "Kashmiri Long Chilli" as a geographical indication in the agricultural class. The file lists a product specification, a historical background, a method of production, a statement of uniqueness and a map of the area; a formality check was recorded on 28 June 2024 and another on 25 September 2025; and when we checked on 17 October 2026 the status was "Pre Examination" ([GI Registry, application 1295](https://search.ipindia.gov.in/GIRPublicSearch/Application/Details/1295)). It sits in the queue a few numbers before the two Wazwan applications, for tabak maaz and gushtaba, filed by The Kashmir Wazwan Cooperative in August 2024 ([GI Registry, application 1336](https://search.ipindia.gov.in/GIRPublicSearch/Application/Details/1336)). None of the three has been examined. If the long chilli's registration is granted, "Kashmiri" on a packet of chilli powder would, for the first time, have to mean something; our [GI guide](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/gi-tag-gushtaba-tabak-maaz) explains how the process works and how to check the register yourself.

## Marchwangan korma and rogan josh

The two red mutton dishes of the Wazwan are easy to confuse and are not the same. The waza Mohammad Shafi Khan draws the line by the cut: marchwangan korma is "a dish made of smaller chunks of meat than used in roganjosh and cooked in a thick chilli gravy" ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)). Rogan josh takes larger pieces on the bone and a gravy whose red comes as much from mawal, the cockscomb flower, as from chilli; marchwangan korma takes smaller pieces and gets its red and its heat from the chilli itself. Outlook Traveller's glossary adds cardamom and coriander to the korma's flavouring ([Outlook Traveller](https://www.outlookindia.com/traveller/ot-getaway-guides/kashmirs-legendary-wazwan-cuisine)); the Gulmarg waza's version was "pot roasted" ([Chef Jolly](https://www.chefjolly.com/culinary-expedition-to-kashmir/)). Both come from the bony portions of the carcass ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/inalienable-ingredient-1499/)). The Srinagar doctors' portions tell the same story from the plate: four pieces of rogan josh to a trami, "4 - 6 pieces" of marchwangan korma ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)). For rogan josh's own story, see [Rogan josh: the history, the name and the red](/blog/rogan-josh-history).

## Mutton, or chicken?

Every reported feast has marchwangan korma as mutton ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258); [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710); [Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html); [The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338); [Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)). Wikipedia's Wazwan article nevertheless describes it in its body as "chicken legs/thighs cooked in a spicy browned-onion sauce", without a citation, while the same article's list calls it "an extremely spicy lamb dish" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan)); the Budgam district administration's page repeats the chicken line ([District Budgam](https://budgam.nic.in/culinary-delight/wazwan/)). A chicken version does exist: Wikipedia's list of Kashmiri dishes records "kokur martsawangan kormeh", citing a Delhi restaurant's menu ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Kashmiri_dishes)). On the evidence, the Wazwan dish is mutton, and the chicken is a restaurant variant. The mutton itself, as our [tabak maaz story](/blog/tabak-maaz-ribs-cooked-twice) sets out, now comes mostly from Rajasthan and the plains: the Valley eats roughly twice the mutton it raises ([Kashmir Times](https://kashmirtimes.com/news/jammu-kashmirs-mutton-crisis-gap-between-demand-and-supply)).

## What it costs, and what cools it

For the hottest dish on the trami, marchwangan korma is a light one. The three doctors from the Sher-i-Kashmir Institute of Medical Sciences who had a wedding's vasta waza "keep a record of every ingredient used" in 2012, and worked the calories out from the National Institute of Nutrition's tables, found the korma carrying 1,059 calories per trami, or 265 for each of the four diners: more than the aab gosh (234) and the single piece of dani (177), less than the rogan josh (331), the tabak maaz (367), the gushtaba (447) and the rista (454). The whole trami came to 21,933 calories with rice, "some 5483 calories to the invited person" ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi, *JMS SKIMS*, 2012](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)). The figures are from one wedding, repeated three times, and the paper is a two-page brief report; it is the only weighed record of the feast we know of. A 2026 public-health advisory in Srinagar, estimating 6,000 to 9,000 calories for a typical trami, named the rice, the meatballs, the kebabs, the tabak maaz and "gravies rich in fats and oils" as the main contributors ([Kashmir Reader](https://kashmirreader.com/2026/06/19/choose-moderation-in-wazwan-as-single-trami-may-contain-6000-9000-calories-health-advisory/)); the korma is a gravy, and it is not named.

What the feast serves against the heat is a bowl of curd. The radish chutney, muji chetin, comes between the heavy dishes to help digestion ([JK Arts Foundation](https://www.jkartsfoundation.in/post/kashmiri-wazwan-celebration-of-food-culture-and-people)); at the wedding Gulf News describes it arrived in the same run of courses as the korma ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710)). Grierson's dictionary had it a century ago as *buröni*, a salad of pounded radishes mixed with curd, seasoned with cumin and salt, and eaten uncooked ([Grierson, p. 124](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=curds&searchhws=no)). The pairing of the Valley's hottest braise with its oldest cold relish is not something any source explains; it is simply what the trami carries.

## Where it comes in the meal

Marchwangan korma comes in the middle, among the heavy red dishes. Outlook India's order has it after rista, rogan josh and daniwal korma, and before aab gosht, yakhni and gushtaba ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258)); at the wedding Gulf News describes it arrived in the same run, with the greens and the radish chutney ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710)). At the wedding the Srinagar doctors recorded it came later still, eleventh of twelve, after the aab gosh and the spinach and immediately before the goshtaba ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)). Kitchens differ on exactly where; they agree that after the korma the feast turns pale, and that yakhni and gushtaba carry no chilli at all. It is the last hot thing you eat. [How Wazwan is served](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/how-wazwan-is-served) gives the whole order.

## Eating it for the first time

In a Srinagar restaurant, marchwangan korma is the dish to ask about before ordering; our [guide to eating in Srinagar](/restaurants/best-wazwan-srinagar) lists places, and the [cost guide](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/cost-guide) covers prices. Expect small pieces of mutton in a thick, dark red gravy, eaten with plenty of rice, and heat that varies from kitchen to kitchen. If you are sensitive to chilli, order rogan josh or daniwal korma instead and taste the korma from a companion's plate. At a wedding, it comes to the trami whether you asked for it or not, the radish chutney is there for a reason, and the yakhni that follows will put the fire out.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is marchwangan korma?

Mutton, in smaller pieces than rogan josh, braised in a thick gravy built on Kashmiri red chilli, with yogurt and spices. It is one of the seven essential dishes of a Wazwan and the hottest.

### What does marchwangan mean?

Chilli. Grierson's dictionary of Kashmiri gives *marʦ* for the red chilli and records the strings of dried chillies kept for winter; the *-wã̄gan-* in the word is the plant element shared with *wã̄gun*, the brinjal. Korma means braise, so the name is chilli-braise.

### How hot is marchwangan korma?

Hotter than anything else on the trami, but by quantity rather than by the chilli's own strength. Kashmiri chilli rates about 1,000 to 2,000 Scoville units, below a mild jalapeño; the dish uses a great deal of it, by one estimate three-quarters of all the chilli in the feast. Kitchens vary, and some "Kashmiri chilli" powders have tested at 48,000 to 60,000 units.

### Where did Kashmir's chilli come from?

From the Americas, by way of the Portuguese, who carried capsicums to Goa and their other Asian ports in the sixteenth century. When the plant reached Kashmir is not recorded; Grierson's dictionary, compiled from the 1890s, already has the word for a string of dried chillies, and Walter Lawrence's 1895 account of the Valley mentions "red pepper" once.

### Why do some "Kashmiri chilli" powders test so hot?

Because the demand outruns the supply and substitutes are used: Byadagi chillies from Karnataka are common stand-ins, and India's Consumer Education and Research Centre found two branded "Kashmiri" powders at 48,000 and 60,000 Scoville units against the true chilli's 1,000 to 2,000. Brick powder has also been recorded as an adulterant. Byadagi has held a geographical indication since 2011; the Kashmiri long chilli's application was filed in 2024 and has not been examined.

### Is marchwangan korma mutton or chicken?

Mutton, in every reported feast and in the wazas' own accounts. Wikipedia's uncited description of chicken legs in a browned-onion sauce is contradicted by its own list; a chicken version, kokur martsawangan kormeh, appears on restaurant menus.

### What is the difference between marchwangan korma and rogan josh?

A waza puts it by the cut: the korma uses smaller chunks than rogan josh, in a thick chilli gravy. Rogan josh takes larger pieces on the bone and gets its red from mawal as well as chilli; the korma gets its red and its heat from the chilli alone.

### How many calories are in marchwangan korma?

At the Srinagar wedding studied by SKIMS doctors in 2012, the korma carried about 1,059 calories per trami, or 265 per diner, less than rogan josh, tabak maaz, gushtaba or rista.

### Does Kashmiri chilli have a GI tag?

Not yet. An application to register "Kashmiri Long Chilli" was filed on 26 June 2024 by a producer company in Baramulla and was at pre-examination when we checked the register on 17 October 2026.

### When is marchwangan korma served at a Wazwan?

In the middle or towards the end of the meat courses, after rista, rogan josh and daniwal korma; before aab gosht in one account and after it in another; always before the pale dishes, yakhni and gushtaba. It is the last hot dish.

## Fact-check list (for editors; not for publication)

Every claim is listed with its source in `../research/07-marchwangan-korma.md`. Points to weigh:

1. **The name.** Grierson's *marʦ* entry (now quoted in full from the site's excerpt: "long pepper, red pepper, a pod of red pepper, a red chilli, Capsicum sp.") and its *-wã̄gan-āra* compound, and his *wã̄gun* (brinjal) entry, are read. The statement that marchwangan is "the Kashmiri word for the chilli pod itself, formed from the chilli and the plant word it shares with the brinjal" is our reading of those two entries and is flagged as such in the text.
2. **"Pepper before chilli"**: the pepper entries (*maga*, *pipijü*, *loṭu*, *nal*, *ʦhaṭh*, *ʦarŏn*) are from the site's "pepper" search excerpts and are summarised, not quoted, except *ʦhaṭh*. The claim that Kashmiri "gave the new pod the old pepper's name" is an inference from the order of senses in the *marʦ* gloss; keep the hedge.
3. **The chilli's route**: Wikipedia's Portuguese-to-Goa sentences are uncited on the page, and the article says so inline. The "Goa connection" paragraph explicitly declines to say how the Kashmiri cultivar arose.
4. **Lawrence**: negative search results ("chillies", "capsicum", "coriander") and the single "red pepper" hit are reported as such; p. 300 is the printed page as read in the OCR text.
5. **"Three-quarters of the chilli"** is an IANS feature's estimate, attributed.
6. **CERC figures** are from Wikipedia's Kashmiri red chilli article, already used on the dish page; the 1999 capsaicin figure (0.126%) is from the same article.
7. **The Scoville section** is from Wikipedia's Scoville scale article; the comparison figures (jalapeño, cayenne) are its table values.
8. **GI 1295** was read on the registry on 15 October 2026; "a farmers' company" describes a "Vegetable Producer Company Limited" and may be adjusted if the owner knows the applicant.
