By the time aab gosht reaches the trami, the red dishes are behind you. Rista, rogan josh and the two kormas have come and gone, and the wazas now bring the pale ones: a piece of mutton in a gravy the colour of cream, then yakhni, then gushtaba ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258)). The cream is milk, and a great deal of it. "Ten litres of pure cow milk are reduced to one and then mixed with the mutton," Mohammad Shafi Khan, a second-generation waza from downtown Srinagar, told *The Kashmir Walla* ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)).

Aab gosht is the gentlest of the seven essential Wazwan dishes and the one with the most misleading name. The name is Persian and means meat broth; the dish is Kashmiri and is made with milk. In Iran a dish of the same name is something else entirely. This story covers the two names, the two dishes, what milk meant in the Valley before anyone wrote the recipe down, the words Kashmiri has for it, the reduction and what it does, the cut, the spices, what the dish costs the body, and where it comes in the meal. For what the dish is, with a recipe and where to eat it, see [aab gosht](/dishes/aab-gosht).

## What aab gosht is

Aab gosht is mutton cooked in reduced milk. Wikipedia describes "lamb chunks cooked with a fennel-based spice mixture, cardamom and partially evaporated milk" and lists it among the seven dishes that are "a must" at a Wazwan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan)); its list of Kashmiri dishes calls it "a famous milk-based curry cooked in spices and ghee over a low flame" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Kashmiri_dishes)). The Waza family's cookbook counts it among the seven indispensable dishes ([Roli Books](https://rolibooks.com/product/wazwaan-traditional-kashmiri-cuisine/)), and a 2016 review of the feast's meat products names it with kabab, tabak maaz, rogan josh, yakhni, rista and goshtaba ([Rather, Masoodi and Akhter](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jef.2016.12.002)). Reporters describe it as "sheep ribs cooked in a milk-based gravy and flavoured with green cardamoms and saffron" ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258)) and, more simply, "mutton in milk sauce" ([Outlook Traveller](https://www.outlookindia.com/traveller/ot-getaway-guides/kashmirs-legendary-wazwan-cuisine)). *The Hindu*'s 2012 account, as Wikipedia quotes it, calls it "lamb cooked in sweet milk curry" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan)). Doctors who recorded a Srinagar wedding feast in 2012 were more anatomical: "1 piece of sacral area of vertebral column prepared in milk", one large piece to a trami ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi, SKIMS, 2012](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)).

## Two names, two dishes

The dish has a Persian name and a Kashmiri one, and they say different things.

**Aab gosht** is Persian. The names of rogan josh, yakhni, aab gosht, rista and gushtaba all "stem from Persian sources", as Wikipedia puts it, citing the 2016 review ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)). In Persian, abgoosht means "literally 'meat broth'" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abgoosht)): *āb*, water, and *gūsht*, meat. When a former head of Kashmir University's Persian department listed the dishes that Persian cooks taught Kashmiris, aab gosht was on it, with kabab, rogan josh, korma, yakhni, pulao, kofta and rista ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338), summarising Shams-ud-din Ahmad's *Shah-i-Hamdan aur Kashmir*). Kashmiri kept the Persian element and used it the Persian way. Grierson's *Dictionary of the Kashmiri Language* has no entry for aab gosht, but under *gōshĕth*, meat, it enters *gusht-āba*, "a rich soup of pounded or minced meat" ([Grierson, p. 309](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=gosht&searchhws=no)): the same two elements in the other order, naming the dish for its liquid. In the Valley's kitchen vocabulary, *āb* meant the broth a thing was cooked in, whatever the broth was made of.

**Dodhe maaz** is Kashmiri, and it describes what is in the pot. *Dŏd* is milk and *māz* is meat in Grierson's dictionary ([Grierson, pp. 190 and 615](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=milk&searchhws=no)): milk-meat. Wikipedia's list of Kashmiri dishes gives dodhe maaz as the dish's other name, and a Srinagar food writer's recipe is titled "Kashmiri Aab Gosh (Dodhe Maaz)" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Kashmiri_dishes); [Rising Kashmir](https://www.risingkashmir.com/recipe-kashmiri-aab-gosh-dodhe-maaz/)). So the Persian name records the idea of a meat broth, and the Kashmiri name records what Kashmir did with it.

What Iran calls abgoosht is a different dish. It is "usually made with lamb, chickpeas, white beans, onion, potatoes, tomatoes, turmeric, and dried lime", a "hearty mutton Persian soup thickened with chickpeas", cooked and then strained, with the broth eaten first with bread and the solids mashed into a paste; it is served in stoneware crocks called *dizi*, which has become its other name ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abgoosht)). No milk, and beans and potatoes that the Kashmiri dish has never had. *Kashmir Life* notes the contrast in standing too: aab gosht is "rich" in Kashmir, but the Iranian dish it is named for began as "poor man's food" ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/kashmir-wazwan-issue-42-vol-12-257951/)). What travelled from Persia, on this evidence, was the name and the idea of meat in its liquid. The milk is Kashmir's.

## Shir and āb: two Persian words, two Kashmiri liquids

There is a second Persian word for a liquid in the Kashmiri kitchen, and it went to a different dish. The salted tea that Kashmiris drink through the day is noon chai, from *noon*, salt, and it is also called sheer chai, from *shir*, which "means 'milk' in Persian"; Kashmiri Pandits call it sheer chai still ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noon_chai)). It is "traditionally made from green tea leaves, milk, salt and baking soda, and is usually cooked in a samavar", the soda turning the brew red and the milk turning the red to pink, and it is "popularly consumed numerous times per day in Kashmir" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noon_chai)). Lawrence met it in the 1890s: tea, he wrote, was served either very sweet, as kahwa, or very salty, as *shir*, with milk, from a samovar, and eaten with wheaten bread ([Lawrence, p. 254](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)).

So Kashmiri took two Persian words for two of its milk preparations and crossed them. The milk tea got *shir*, milk; the milk curry got *āb*, water. That is not a muddle so much as a record of what each name was describing: the tea is named for what is added to it, the dish for the Persian category it was understood to belong to, meat in a broth. The Kashmiri names, dodhe maaz and noon chai, name the ingredient and the salt. Nobody has recorded when either Persian word attached itself, and we do not propose a date; the dictionary, the tea and the dish simply arrive together in the nineteenth-century record, with the words already in place.

## Milk in the Valley, 1895

Why milk? The sources give no origin for the dish, so the honest answer is that nobody knows. But the Valley that made it was, by the one detailed account we have of its food before the twentieth century, a place awash in milk and short of almost every other richness.

Walter Lawrence, the state's settlement commissioner, wrote in 1895 that the cultivator's diet was rice and other grains, vegetables, oil, salt and pepper, and milk without limit; that fowl was eaten about six times a month and mutton "perhaps five times in the month"; and that "clarified butter (ghi) is not much eaten", because Kashmiris believed it irritated the throat ([Lawrence, pp. 253 to 254](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). Elsewhere he records that Kashmiris depended heavily on milk and wool, kept their cattle in byres on the ground floor of the house, and thought maize unpalatable without milk; that in the famine of 1878 people had lived on wild herbs "while skim milk lasted", and that herbs without milk proved fatal; that goat's milk was reckoned strengthening and milch goats were led from door to door to feed infants; and that tea was always taken with milk, salted or sweet ([Lawrence, *The Valley of Kashmir*](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr), found through the archive's full-text search). The cows themselves were small. A cow giving four seers of milk a day, about four kilograms, was treasured; improved cows gave eight; rice straw and rice water were said to raise the yield; and a proverb warned that loud lowing and a large udder rarely meant much milk ([Lawrence, chapter on cattle](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). The ghi the Valley did make was mostly from buffalo milk, made by the Gujar herders and sold to Punjabi middlemen, and was by far its most important export; cow's-milk ghi, Lawrence noted, was reckoned the most delicate ([Lawrence, chapters on cattle and exports](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)).

Much of that milk came down from the hills with the herders. Lawrence records the Gujars giving away all of Friday's milk in charity; the Gujars and Pathans of the Lolab making a cheese he calls milk-bread, which he thought could supply the garrisons of upper India with cheese and butter; the village shepherd, the *chaupan*, taking as a perquisite the butter he made from sheep's milk; and the state taxing milch buffaloes for their summer grazing in the forests ([Lawrence, chapter on cattle and the pages on the Gujars](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). The same transhumant communities that bring the Valley its sheep in autumn, as our [tabak maaz story](/blog/tabak-maaz-ribs-cooked-twice) describes, were its dairy in Lawrence's day. A wedding in October, when the flocks come down, is a wedding in the season of both.

Milk was ritual as well as food. Lawrence records a feast of rice and milk at a child's first month, rice boiled in milk as the first solid food at six months, rice boiled in milk distributed when smallpox abated, and, at the spring of Khir Bhawani, the "milk goddess" of the Kashmiri Pandits, offerings of sugar, milk, rice and flowers; Hindus visiting her abstained from meat that day ([Lawrence, pp. 260, 286 and 300](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). Grierson's dictionary, compiled in the same decades, has a matching omen: *zang*, a sign of good luck, its example "a dish of curds" placed on the right hand before starting an undertaking ([Grierson, p. 1227](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=curds&searchhws=no)). A dish that cooks the feast's mutton in ten litres of milk belongs to that Valley: one where milk was the ordinary abundance, ghee was something you sold, and a bowl of white was a good sign. That is context, not a cause; no source says the wazas chose milk for any of these reasons.

## The words for milk

Kashmiri distinguishes the milk in aab gosht from the curd in yakhni and gushtaba, and the dictionary shows how fine the distinctions run. *Dŏd* is milk ([Grierson, p. 190](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=milk&searchhws=no)). *Zyonu* is to curdle, and *zyonu dŏd* is curdled milk; *caka* is curds, curdled milk with the whey strained out; *mana* is "whey, the liquid of curds"; *har* is the scum that rises like cream on curds; *aḍ* is milk only half boiled; *chôpu* is the small churning-stick ([Grierson, pp. 1239, 168, 572, 343, 12 and 163](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=curdled&searchhws=no)). The dishes follow the words. *Yĕkhañĕ* is "a kind of dish (consisting of meat cooked with spices in curdled milk)" ([Grierson, p. 1192](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=milk&searchhws=no)); *kürü* is "curdled milk cooked and spiced, used as a relish with other food"; *trakānüja* is "a drink made up of milk, curds, and water mixed in equal parts"; *buröni* is a salad of pounded radish in curds with cumin and salt, which is the radish chutney a Wazwan still serves between its heavy dishes ([Grierson, pp. 468, 1024 and 124](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=curds&searchhws=no)). What the dictionary does not record is any dish of meat cooked in fresh milk. Aab gosht, or dodhe maaz, is not there. Whether that means the dish is younger than the dictionary's informants, or only that no informant mentioned it, the dictionary cannot say; it records a kitchen in which milk was everywhere and curd was the usual medium for meat.

## A dish with a short paper trail

For a dish counted among the seven, aab gosht has left few marks on paper, and the gaps are themselves informative. Grierson's dictionary, compiled from the 1890s, has words for milk, curds, whey and a meat dish cooked in curd, and none for a meat dish cooked in milk. The 1988 *Culinary Art of Kashmir*, written from the Kashmiri Pandit kitchen with a separate section for the cuisine of the Valley's Muslims, has yakhni twice in its index and no dish of meat in milk at all ([Kaul Kilam, index](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/index2.html)). The first written places we find the dish are the wazas' own canon, the Roli cookbook's list of seven indispensable dishes ([Roli Books](https://rolibooks.com/product/wazwaan-traditional-kashmiri-cuisine/)), the 2016 food-science review that names it among the feast's ethnic meat products ([Rather, Masoodi and Akhter](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jef.2016.12.002)), and the 2012 medical paper that weighed it ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)); Wikipedia's own article on it is a stub, and the one full recipe in a Kashmiri newspaper dates from 2023 ([Rising Kashmir](https://www.risingkashmir.com/recipe-kashmiri-aab-gosh-dodhe-maaz/)). None of that proves the dish is young; it proves that it belongs to the wazas' kitchen, which was not written down, rather than to the Pandit one, which was. Our [history of Wazwan](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/history-of-wazwan) sets out how little of the feast's past is on paper at all.

## The milk: ten to one

The defining act of the dish is a reduction. Shafi Khan's ten litres to one is matched by Wikipedia's list, which records the same figure ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Kashmiri_dishes)). A chef who cooked with a Gulmarg waza describes fresh milk reduced "with cinnamon, green cardamom, paste of praan, garlic, ginger powder and fennel powder", the boiled meat then simmered in the milk sauce "until the flavour becomes well-rounded" ([Chef Jolly](https://www.chefjolly.com/culinary-expedition-to-kashmir/)).

Ten to one is a long way to take milk. The nearest comparison in South Asian cooking is khoa, the milk solid that is the base of most of the subcontinent's sweets, made "by simmering full-fat milk in a large, shallow iron pan for several hours over a medium fire", at about 80 °C to avoid scorching, and for which "a concentration of milk to one-fifth volume is normal" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoa)). The waza's reduction goes twice as far as the sweet-maker's, and does it in a copper deg over wood, with the aromatics in the milk from the start. Nobody has recorded how long it takes a wedding kitchen; the sources say only that the cooking begins around three in the morning and runs for about six hours ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html); [The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)).

Reducing ten litres of milk takes a wood fire and a morning, so most published recipes take a shortcut: milk powder. Sahapedia's account of the wazas' dish has full-cream dry milk added to the mutton stock, with ghee to finish, giving "a creamy soup" ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)); the Srinagar recipe uses 500 g of full-cream dry milk to a kilo of mutton, mixed with two or three cups of water or stock and brought to the boil before the boiled meat goes in, with two more cups of stock, fried shallot paste and ghee, and a scatter of almonds at the end ([Rising Kashmir](https://www.risingkashmir.com/recipe-kashmiri-aab-gosh-dodhe-maaz/)). Wikipedia's "partially evaporated milk" describes the same thing from the other direction ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan)). Khoa and milk powder are, in the end, the same shortcut: milk with the water already taken out.

## Milk in a copper pot

The reduction happens in the same pots as everything else. At the wedding Outlook Traveller watched in 2017, "in the kitchen tent, a dozen men worked over a raging fire fed by whole logs arranged along a trench", and "on it simmered enormous brass vats bearing aab gosht, rogan josh, rista, pulao" ([Outlook Traveller](https://www.outlookindia.com/traveller/ot-getaway-guides/kashmirs-legendary-wazwan-cuisine)). Other accounts call the vats copper: "nickel-plated copper daegs" in Outlook India, nickel-plated copper on fruit-tree logs in IANS's account ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258); [Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)). The word is in Grierson's dictionary as *dēg*, "a large pile of rice cooked in a cauldron, for a wedding feast or the like", the cauldron having given its name to what was cooked in it ([Grierson, p. 194](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=cauldron&searchhws=no)).

The plating matters more for this dish than for any other on the trami, and the dictionary says why. Its entry for *zangāra*, verdigris, notes that milk or curds in a metal vessel spoil under a verdigris scum ([Grierson, p. 1227](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=curds&searchhws=no)). A hundred years before anyone wrote "nickel-plated", Kashmiri already had a word for what bare copper does to milk. Ten litres reduced to one in a pot that is not lined would not be aab gosht; it would be the dictionary's *zangāra*. The lining, the tinning that Kashmiri coppersmiths renew and the nickel that has replaced it in modern degs, is the unglamorous technology the dish depends on. Our [history of Wazwan](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/history-of-wazwan) follows the copper.

The sweetness that *The Hindu* noticed comes without sugar. Reduced milk is sweet in itself, and Khan adds cloves sautéed in pure ghee "for sweetness" ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)). None of the accounts we have read adds chilli, tomato or turmeric; the dish is pale because nothing in it is red. Lawrence noted that Kashmiris liked their food very salty and were "very fond of tea and sugar", and that tea came either very sweet or salted ([Lawrence, p. 254](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)); a sweetish, salted milk gravy would not have surprised him.

## Milk today

The Valley Lawrence described still milks small cows, and many more of them. Jammu and Kashmir has "1.76 million non-descript indigenous cattle" and "1.05 million buffaloes", according to a 2022 review by the Sher-e-Kashmir agricultural university, which quotes Lawrence's own description of the local cattle as "small, hardy and conservative in habits" ([Hamadani and others, *Indian Journal of Animal Sciences*, 2022](https://doi.org/10.56093/ijans.v92i4.124009)). The union government told the Lok Sabha in 2026 that the territory's milk production had risen from 2.82 million tonnes in 2022-23 to 2.97 million tonnes in 2024-25, and that the milk available per person had risen from 572 to 595 grams a day, against a national figure of 485 ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/jammu-kashmir-milk-production-rises-to-2-97-million-tonnes-lok-sabha-told-446548/)). Where the Valley imports more than half its mutton, as our [tabak maaz story](/blog/tabak-maaz-ribs-cooked-twice) sets out, its milk is its own. Of the two main ingredients of aab gosht, the ten litres are the local one.

## The cut and the spices

The sources name different cuts, and all of them are big. Sahapedia has meat from the lamb's tail, shoulder or shank, boiled until tender ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)); the Srinagar recipe has the same three ([Rising Kashmir](https://www.risingkashmir.com/recipe-kashmiri-aab-gosh-dodhe-maaz/)). Khan uses "one large piece from the lower back", slow-cooked in the milk ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)); the Srinagar doctors' "sacral area of vertebral column" is the same place in a surgeon's words ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)). Two reporters say sheep ribs ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258); [Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)). What they share is a single large piece per diner rather than small chunks, boiled first and finished in the milk.

The spices are the Wazwan's gentle register. Sahapedia's list is shallot, garlic paste, ginger powder, fennel powder, cloves, green and black cardamom, cinnamon and salt ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)); the Srinagar recipe's is the same with fried shallot paste and ghee ([Rising Kashmir](https://www.risingkashmir.com/recipe-kashmiri-aab-gosh-dodhe-maaz/)). Wikipedia calls the mixture "fennel-based" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan)), and two accounts add saffron with the green cardamom ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258); [Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)). Grierson's dictionary has the two foundation spices as *bödiyāna*, sweet fennel, whose kernels are pounded in a mortar, and *shō̃ṭh*, dry ginger ([Grierson, pp. 89 and 899](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=fennel&searchhws=no)); see [fennel and dry ginger](/blog/fennel-and-dry-ginger-wazwan).

## What it costs

For a dish built on ten litres of milk, aab gosht is the lightest meat on the trami. The three doctors from the Sher-i-Kashmir Institute of Medical Sciences who had a wedding's vasta waza "keep a record of every ingredient used" in 2012, and worked the calories out from the National Institute of Nutrition's tables, found the aab gosh carrying 934 calories per trami, or 234 for each of the four diners: less than the marchwangan korma (265), the rogan josh (331), the tabak maaz (367), the gushtaba (447) and the rista (454), and less than everything else they weighed except the single piece of dani (177). The whole trami came to 21,933 calories with rice, "some 5483 calories to the invited person" ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi, *JMS SKIMS*, 2012](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)). The figures are from one wedding, repeated three times, and the paper is a two-page brief report; but they are the only weighed record of the feast we know of, and they put the milk dish where a diner would expect it, at the mild end. A 2026 public-health advisory in Srinagar, estimating 6,000 to 9,000 calories for a typical trami, named the rice, the meatballs, the kebabs, the tabak maaz and "gravies rich in fats and oils" as the main contributors, and did not name aab gosht at all ([Kashmir Reader](https://kashmirreader.com/2026/06/19/choose-moderation-in-wazwan-as-single-trami-may-contain-6000-9000-calories-health-advisory/)).

## Where it comes in the meal

Aab gosht comes late. In Outlook India's account of the order it follows marchwangan korma and comes before yakhni and gushtaba ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258)); in Asia Media Centre's it follows rista, rogan josh and daniphol ([Asia Media Centre](https://www.asiamediacentre.org.nz/features/the-intricacies-of-kashmiri-wazwan)). At the wedding the Srinagar doctors recorded it came ninth of eleven dishes, after the tomato paneer and before the spinach, the marchwangan korma and the goshtaba ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)), which is a reminder that kitchens differ in the middle of the meal even as they agree on its ends. It is served on the trami with rice ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)). Read across the accounts, the feast moves from red to white: the chilli-coloured dishes in the middle, then aab gosht, yakhni and gushtaba, each paler and milder than the last. That shape is our observation; the order is the sources'. [How Wazwan is served](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/how-wazwan-is-served) gives the whole sequence.

## Eating it for the first time

Most visitors meet aab gosht in a Srinagar restaurant, on its own or late in a set trami; our [guide to eating in Srinagar](/restaurants/best-wazwan-srinagar) lists places, and the [cost guide](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/cost-guide) covers prices. Expect a large piece of mutton in a pale, creamy gravy with the scent of fennel and cardamom and a faint sweetness, eaten with rice. It is mild; there is no chilli in it. And if you have eaten abgoosht in an Iranian restaurant, expect nothing like it.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is aab gosht?

Mutton, usually a large piece from the shoulder, shank, tail, lower back or ribs, boiled and then cooked in milk reduced with fennel, cardamom, cloves, cinnamon, ginger, shallot and garlic. It is one of the seven essential dishes of a Wazwan and comes late in the meal.

### What does aab gosht mean?

The name is Persian and means meat broth: *āb*, water, and *gūsht*, meat. Its Kashmiri name, dodhe maaz, means milk-meat, which is what the dish is.

### Is aab gosht the same as Persian abgoosht?

No. The Iranian abgoosht is lamb with chickpeas, beans, onion, potatoes, tomatoes, turmeric and dried lime, strained and mashed, and served in a stone pot. The Kashmiri dish shares the name and nothing else: it is mutton in reduced milk with fennel and cardamom.

### How much milk goes into aab gosht?

A waza describes ten litres of cow's milk reduced to one for the wedding dish, twice the reduction used to make khoa for sweets. Published home recipes use full-cream milk powder instead, about 500 g to a kilo of mutton.

### Is aab gosht made with milk or yogurt?

Fresh milk. Kashmiri keeps the two apart: *dŏd* is milk and *caka* or *zyonu dŏd* is curd. Yakhni and gushtaba are cooked in curd; aab gosht, or dodhe maaz, is the Wazwan's one dish cooked in reduced fresh milk.

### Why is aab gosht sweet?

Because reduced milk is sweet in itself; no sugar is added. A Srinagar waza also sautés cloves in ghee "for sweetness". *The Hindu* described the dish as "lamb cooked in sweet milk curry".

### Is aab gosht cooked in copper?

In nickel-plated or tinned copper degs over wood, like the rest of the feast; one reporter saw brass vats. Grierson's dictionary records that milk or curds spoil in a bare metal vessel under a scum of verdigris, which is why the lining matters more for this dish than for any other.

### Is there a Kashmiri Pandit aab gosht?

We have found none. The 1988 Pandit cookbook's index lists no dish of meat cooked in milk, and Grierson's dictionary records no such dish either; aab gosht appears in writing in the wazas' own list of seven and in later reporting, which places it with the Muslim feast rather than the Pandit table.

### Which cut of meat is used?

Large pieces: tail, shoulder or shank in Sahapedia's account, one large piece from the lower back in a waza's, "the sacral area of the vertebral column" in a medical paper's, sheep ribs in two reporters'. Each diner gets one piece.

### How many calories are in aab gosht?

At the Srinagar wedding studied by SKIMS doctors in 2012, the aab gosh carried about 934 calories per trami, or 234 per diner, the lightest of the main mutton dishes they weighed.

### Is aab gosht spicy?

No. Its flavour is fennel, cardamom, cinnamon, cloves and ginger in reduced milk, with saffron in some accounts. None of the accounts we have read adds chilli, tomato or turmeric.

### When is aab gosht served at a Wazwan?

Late, among the pale dishes: after marchwangan korma and before yakhni and gushtaba in one account; after rista, rogan josh and daniphol in another; before the korma and the gushtaba at the wedding a medical team recorded.

## Fact-check list (for editors; not for publication)

Every claim is listed with its source in `../research/05-aab-gosht.md`. Points to weigh:

1. **"Meat broth."** Wikipedia's Abgoosht article gives the literal translation; the split into *āb* and *gūsht* is the ordinary reading of the Persian words and is stated without a further source. The Abgoosht page's preparation section is tagged "citation needed" on Wikipedia; the article uses it only to describe the Iranian dish in general terms. The sentence "in the Valley's kitchen vocabulary, *āb* meant the broth a thing was cooked in" is our reading of Grierson's *gusht-āba* entry.
2. **"Rich" versus "poor man's food"** is *Kashmir Life*'s remark, attributed.
3. **Chef Jolly** is a personal blog, attributed as such.
4. **"From red to white"** is marked as our observation; so is the "context, not a cause" paragraph on milk in 1895, which says explicitly that no source gives a reason for the milk.
5. **Lawrence's dairy material** was found through the Internet Archive's full-text search of the 1895 scan; the snippets do not show printed page numbers for the cattle and export chapters, so those are cited by chapter. The food, birth-custom, smallpox and Khir Bhawani passages are cited to printed pages read in the OCR text (253 to 254, 260, 286, 300); the birth and smallpox page numbers are inferred from the scan's leaf numbering (about 22 higher than the printed page in this part of the book) and should be checked against a physical copy if precision matters.
6. **"Four seers, about four kilograms"**: a seer in Kashmir in Lawrence's time was close to a kilogram, but the conversion is approximate and is given as such.
7. **The dictionary's silence on aab gosht** is a negative result from the site's search ("dodhe", "aab" and "milk" searches); the article says the dictionary cannot tell us why.
8. **The SKIMS figures** are from one wedding, repeated three times, as stated; the paper's "sacral area of vertebral column" is quoted as written.
9. The dish page's note that no account adds chilli, tomato or turmeric was checked against the same sources on 12 October 2026 and again here.
10. **Outlook Traveller's byline.** The page credits Benny Joshua, 28 July 2017; the earlier draft credited Rimli Sengupta, which does not appear on the page. Corrected.
11. **Shir and āb.** The Persian meanings (*shir* milk, *āb* water) are from Wikipedia's Noon chai and Abgoosht articles; the "crossed them" observation is ours and is framed as such, with an explicit refusal to date it.
12. **Zangāra.** The verdigris entry is from the Digital South Asia Library's "curds" search excerpt and is paraphrased; the inference about tinning and nickel plating is ours, resting on the reporters' "nickel-plated copper".
13. **The Gujar dairy paragraph** rests on archive full-text search snippets (scan pages 344, 398, 400 and 434) and is cited by chapter.
