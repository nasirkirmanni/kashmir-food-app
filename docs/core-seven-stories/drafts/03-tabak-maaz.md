The trami comes to the four of you under a copper cover, the sarposh ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258)). When it is lifted, the rice is already dressed: two seekh kebabs crossing it into quarters, four pieces of methi maaz, two chickens, and two pieces of tabak maaz ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan); [District Budgam](https://budgam.nic.in/culinary-delight/wazwan/)). At the wedding a Gulf News writer attended, the guests had "less than ten minutes to finish it off" before the cooked courses began arriving ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710)). So the first thing most people ever taste of a Wazwan is a piece of rib: simmered until the bones slip, then fried in ghee until it is "reddish brown all over" ([Scroll, from the Waza family's cookbook](https://scroll.in/food/945552/tabakh-maaz)).

Tabak maaz is the plainest of the seven essential dishes and the one with the most disagreement about how it is made. It is cooked twice; on that everyone agrees. What it is simmered in before the frying, whether it is the same dish as the Kashmiri Pandits' kabargah, what its name has to do with a plate, where the sheep whose ribs these are come from, and why a people who once avoided ghee fry their first wedding dish in it are the questions this story works through. For what the dish is, with a recipe and where to eat it, see [tabak maaz](/dishes/tabak-maaz); for the application to register it as a geographical indication, see the [GI guide](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/gi-tag-gushtaba-tabak-maaz).

## What tabak maaz is

Tabak maaz is lamb ribs cooked twice: "initially braised with ground spices, then browned in ghee", in Wikipedia's summary ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan)). It is one of the seven dishes Wikipedia lists as "a must" at a Wazwan and one of the seven the Waza family's cookbook calls indispensable ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan); [Roli Books](https://rolibooks.com/product/wazwaan-traditional-kashmiri-cuisine/)), and a 2016 review of the feast's meat products names it with kabab, aab gosh, rogan josh, yakhni, rista and goshtaba ([Rather, Masoodi and Akhter](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jef.2016.12.002)). Unlike the gravies that follow it, it comes dry: "a glossy meat made of lamb ribs, fried thoroughly and served in dry form" ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258)). Doctors who recorded a Srinagar wedding feast in 2012 described it the same way, "made up of rib cage of sheep, cooked in ghee and served as dry pieces", two to four pieces to a trami ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi, SKIMS, 2012](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)). In August 2024 The Kashmir Wazwan Cooperative Limited applied to register "Tabakmaaz (Meat Products) of Kashmir" as a geographical indication; on 15 October 2026 the application was still at pre-examination ([GI Registry, application 1336](https://search.ipindia.gov.in/GIRPublicSearch/Application/Details/1336)).

## The plate in the name

Kashmiri has words for ribs. Grierson's *Dictionary of the Kashmiri Language* gives *pōwaca*, "the ribs (of man or beast)", *pāha-lara*, "the ribs (spoken of as a whole)", and *lar*, "a bone of the side, a rib" ([Grierson, pp. 810, 684, 529](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=rib&searchhws=no)). The dish uses none of them. *Māz* is meat ([Grierson, p. 615](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=maz&searchhws=yes)), and *tabakh* is a large dish, plate or tray ([Grierson, p. 962](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=tabak&searchhws=yes)). Tabak maaz is, literally, plate-meat.

Which plate is not recorded, but two threads lead somewhere. The first is old. *Kashmir Life*, summarising the *Tuhfat-ul-Ahbab*, a Persian work completed in the early 17th century, describes 16th-century feasts at which food was served on a dastarkhwan in a copper *tabak*, "maybe a forerunner of traem of today", and at grand ceremonies the tabak came "covered with sarposh" ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/kashmir-wazwan-issue-42-vol-12-257951/)). The covered copper plate the modern trami arrives under had a name four centuries ago, and it was tabak.

The second thread is a recipe. A Kashmiri cookbook first published in 1988 gives one method under the double name "Kabargah Ta Tabaq Maz", and its tabaq maz is cooked in plates. The simmered, battered ribs are laid in a ghee-smeared baked clay plate, a *tabaq*, covered with a second plate, the rims sealed with wheat-flour dough, and the sealed plates set over a slow charcoal fire with coals on top for about an hour, until the meat is deep brown; the earthenware, the book says, gives the dish a special flavour and aroma, and it "should always be served steaming hot" ([Kaul Kilam, *Culinary Art of Kashmir*](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/44.html)). It is the only method we have found that puts a plate into the cooking rather than the serving. Whether the dish was named for the clay plates it was once cooked between, or for the copper plate it was served on, nobody has written down; both fit the word. The only other origin claim we found is a single remark by the former tourism director M. Saleem Beg that the dish "seems to have travelled from Kazakhstan", offered without evidence ([Daily Excelsior](https://www.dailyexcelsior.com/cuisines-of-kashmir-a-tradition-and-a-treasure-trove/)).

The two materials, clay and copper, divide along a line the dictionary draws. Grierson's entry for *trömi* is "a copper dish (used only by Musalmāns) out of which food is eaten", and *trāmi-bata* is the food served in those copper vessels to the Muslim guests at a wedding feast; a separate word, *ṭôku*, covers food served to each guest on a platter of their own ([Grierson, pp. 1026 and 991](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=copper&searchhws=no)). Walter Lawrence, writing in 1895, saw the other material on the table. When "the professional cook is called in some taste is displayed in the preparation and arrangement of viands", he wrote, and "the earthenware platters filled with brightly-coloured foods make a pretty show"; elsewhere in the book he notes that rough earthenware supplied all the wants of the Kashmiri peasant, metal being seldom used for household purposes ([Lawrence, p. 253](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). So the Valley of the 1890s ate off clay and feasted, when Muslim and able, off copper; a dish named for a plate had both to choose from. Our [history of Wazwan](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/history-of-wazwan) follows the copper.

The spelling varies with the writer: tabakh maaz on Wikipedia, tabaq maz in the cookbook, tabakmaz in Beg's essay, "Tabakmaaz" in the doctors' paper and on the GI register. The Kashmiri Pandit name for the dish is kabargah, also written qabargah ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Kashmiri_dishes); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabak_maaz)).

## The sheep

Every account of tabak maaz starts with a rib cage, and in Kashmir the rib cage starts a long way from the kitchen. The Valley has kept sheep for as long as anyone has written about it, and it has never kept enough.

Lawrence was clear about the quality. "The choicest mutton in Kashmir is raised in the village of Nandpur on the Dal lake", where the sheep grazed the tender grass of the made land called *demb*; "the mutton of Kashmir is throughout excellent"; the Kashmiri "considers two-year-old wether mutton as the best"; and the sheep of the valley "have been pronounced by a competent authority to be as good as the Southdowns" ([Lawrence, p. 363](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). He was equally clear about the quantity. "In spite of the number of sheep in Kashmir it is often difficult to obtain mutton in the villages", because villagers kept their flocks for wool and for warmth, penned on the ground floor of the house through the winter, and sold them to the city butchers rather than eating them; and a sheep that sickened in the close winter pens was killed and eaten by its owner as braxy mutton ([Lawrence, pp. 360 to 363](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). The cultivator, he reckoned, ate mutton "perhaps five times in the month" ([Lawrence, p. 254](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)).

The sheep he described still exist, and are now counted as breeds. A 2022 review by animal scientists at the Sher-e-Kashmir agricultural university lists the Valley's own, with their standing: the Gurez, "registered (with NBAGR)", found "only in Gurez and Tulail Tehsils of the Bandipora district", reared by the Dard people, known for "good-quality mutton" and "the biggest sheep breed of Kashmir"; the Karnah, of the Kupwara hills, "under the threat" of disappearing through random cross-breeding; the Poonchi, "primarily reared for both mutton and wool"; and the Bhakarwal, the migratory sheep "reared mainly by the nomadic tribe called the Bhakarwals", hardy, good climbers, some of them fat-tailed ([Hamadani and others, *Indian Journal of Animal Sciences*, 2022](https://doi.org/10.56093/ijans.v92i4.124009)). Together Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh had 8.32 million and 7.37 million livestock and poultry at the 20th Livestock Census, of which sheep were 36.84 per cent, and the two territories "stand 6th with regard to the population of sheep" in India ([Hamadani and others, 2022](https://doi.org/10.56093/ijans.v92i4.124009)). The Kashmir region's sheep alone numbered 3.4 million at the previous census ([Rural21](https://www.rural21.com/english/archive/2019/02/detail/article/the-silent-sheep-revolution.html)).

Most of those sheep are no longer the sheep Lawrence saw. From the 1960s the state's sheep husbandry department crossed Valley ewes with imported rams to make a new animal: "Kashmir valley ewes were crossed with Australian Merino rams", the first-generation ewes "were bred to Delaine rams of the USA", and the second generation was bred among itself "after proper selection" until "a breed with steady and uniform characters evolved which was named as Kashmir Merino" ([Rural21](https://www.rural21.com/english/archive/2019/02/detail/article/the-silent-sheep-revolution.html)). The department's director, Abdul Salam Mir, told a reporter in 2019 that "the quality of sheep which we had before 1990s, used to be low on both meat and wool", and that the Merino was "far more productive both in terms of mutton and wool"; his department ran eight breeding farms and had distributed about 29,000 rams into private flocks ([Village Square](https://villagesquare.in/sheepherders-conserve-kashmir-merino-stock/); [Rural21](https://www.rural21.com/english/archive/2019/02/detail/article/the-silent-sheep-revolution.html)). The 2022 review puts the result in a sentence: "Nearly 43% sheep have already been converted into crossbred", and about 80 per cent of the native breeds were being crossed with exotic fine-wool sheep, which is why the Karnah and the Gurez are now listed as endangered ([Hamadani and others, 2022](https://doi.org/10.56093/ijans.v92i4.124009)). The rib on the trami, in other words, is as likely to come from a sheep that is half Australian and a quarter American as from one Lawrence would have recognised.

It is more likely still to come from Rajasthan. The government's own figures, tabled in the state's legislative council and reported in 2012, put the Valley's mutton consumption at 2,53,88,000 kg a year, worth Rs 761 crore, of which 1,44,08,000 kg was imported, "most of the mutton imports" from Rajasthan: 56 per cent of the meat from outside ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/kashmir-yearly-mutton-consumption-worth-rs-761-64-crore-6744/)). Two years later a *Kashmir Life* editor wrote that "the state's overall requirement for mutton has crossed 560 lakh kilograms", that "the government said that 55% of this volume is net import", and that "the voracious mutton eating habits have given the Wazwaan country a new identity"; the sheep, he added, were "stuffed in trucks beyond capacity" and "for most of the distance between Rajasthan to Srinagar, they are kept hungry and thirsty" ([R. S. Gull, *Kashmir Life*](https://kashmirlife.net/dear-faithful-mutton-eater-issue21-vol06-62854/)). In 2019 the sheep husbandry director gave demand as 31 million kg and imports as 17.7 million kg, "well over 50% of mutton from outside the state" ([Village Square](https://villagesquare.in/sheepherders-conserve-kashmir-merino-stock/)). In 2021 the department's data showed about 22 lakh sheep slaughtered a year in the Valley and "a meager 6.5 lakh sheep are raised locally", so that local production was "just 30% of the total consumption"; the director blamed "harsh winter for six months", which made it "very difficult for the farmers to raise sheep on a big scale" ([Greater Kashmir](https://www.greaterkashmir.com/article/kashmir-consumes-22-lakh-sheep-annually-only-6-5-lakh-raised-locally/59089)). And in February 2026 a three-part series in *Kashmir Times* put local production at 30,000 tonnes against consumption of "around 60,000 tonnes annually", filled by "approximately 14 lakh sheep and goats annually" from Rajasthan, Haryana, Gujarat and Uttar Pradesh, draining "between Rs 1,000 and Rs 1,400 crore" a year; the carcasses of the imported breeds weigh "around 12 kilograms", and the whole trade travels one road, the Jammu-Srinagar highway, which landslides, weather and security closures can cut ([Rao Farman Ali, *Kashmir Times*](https://kashmirtimes.com/news/jammu-kashmirs-mutton-crisis-gap-between-demand-and-supply)). A mutton dealers' office-bearer named the supply chain: Ujlan-Jaisalmer, Jaipal Basti, Jaisalmer and Sikar, in Rajasthan ([Rao Farman Ali, *Kashmir Times*](https://kashmirtimes.com/news/jammu-kashmirs-mutton-crisis-gap-between-demand-and-supply)).

The figures do not agree with one another, and they should not be expected to: they come from different years, different departments and different definitions of "Kashmir". What they agree on is the shape. The Valley eats roughly twice the mutton it raises, has done so for at least fifteen years, and buys the difference from the plains. The reasons given are the same in 1895 and 2026: a six-month winter, flocks kept for wool rather than meat, and pastures that are shrinking ([Lawrence, p. 363](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr); [Kashmir Times](https://kashmirtimes.com/news/jammu-kashmirs-mutton-crisis-gap-between-demand-and-supply)). The herders who move sheep between those pastures, the Bakarwals and Gujjars, sell to the dealers on their autumn migration down from the high meadows, which is also the wedding season ([Greater Kashmir](https://www.greaterkashmir.com/article/kashmir-consumes-22-lakh-sheep-annually-only-6-5-lakh-raised-locally/59089)); the 2012 figures were accompanied by the complaint that most of the meadows along the Line of Control had been closed to herdsmen "mostly from Pir Panchal region" of Rajouri and Poonch ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/kashmir-yearly-mutton-consumption-worth-rs-761-64-crore-6744/)).

What that means for a wedding became plain in the summer of 2026. "Around 500 kilogrammes of mutton and 150 kilogrammes of chicken are consumed at an average Kashmiri wedding", the Press Trust of India reported, and more than a tonne at a lavish one; when Punjab levied a four per cent charge on livestock trucks crossing the state, which the president of the Valley's mutton dealers' association called a "Goonda Tax", the dealers suspended imports, a Budgam dealer who had paid his supplier in Rajasthan said he had "not received any sheep in the past month", and a father in Hyderpora with a son's wedding fixed for 25 July said, "We might have to postpone it" ([PTI via Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/news/mutton-shortage-clouds-kashmiri-weddings-traders-blame-punjab)). The chief minister wrote to his counterpart in Punjab; the levy was withdrawn within days ([Daily Pioneer](https://dailypioneer.com/news/punjab-ends-livestock-tax-ending-kashmir-mutton-crisis)). A veteran waza put the quantities in his own terms to Reuters that autumn: "Normally, at an average marriage we cook 200 kgs of mutton"; forty years earlier, at a village wedding in Delina, he had cooked 4,500 kg ([Reuters](https://kfgo.com/?p=1357989)). Two pieces of tabak maaz to a trami, and a hundred tramis to a wedding, is a great many rib cages; and almost none of them, now, from Nandpur on the Dal.

## The first cooking: water, milk or yogurt?

Every source has the ribs simmered before they are fried. They do not agree on what in.

**Water, say the wazas.** The one published recipe from a waza family, in the Roli Books cookbook *Wazwaan*, uses water and nothing richer. The whole rib cage goes into boiling water and is skimmed "until the water runs clear"; garlic goes in, then salt, and the ribs boil, covered, until the membrane between them can be pierced with a thumb. They are drained, cooled and washed, and the water is kept. Only then is the rack cut, with a heavy knife, into eight rectangular pieces, which go back into the reserved water with salt, dry ginger, cloves, black cardamom and turmeric "until the bones can be extracted from the membrane easily" ([Scroll, from *Wazwaan*](https://scroll.in/food/945552/tabakh-maaz)). A Srinagar waza describes the same first step, whole ribs boiled with salt and garlic ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)), and Wikipedia's list of Kashmiri dishes has the Wazwan version "boiled with salt and garlic" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Kashmiri_dishes)). Sahapedia's account has fatty ribs simmered with cardamom, cinnamon, ginger powder, garlic paste and cloves ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)). A chef's notes from a cooking session with a Gulmarg waza have the rib cage boiled for about two hours in stock with turmeric, fennel seeds, cardamom, cinnamon and salt ([Chef Jolly](https://www.chefjolly.com/culinary-expedition-to-kashmir/)).

**Milk, say others.** Wikipedia's own article on the dish has the ribs "simmered in milk and spices and then fried" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabak_maaz)); a news-agency feature has them "cooked in milk and then fried in clarified butter" ([Anadolu Agency](https://aa.com.tr/en/asia-pacific/winning-hearts-and-minds-through-famed-kashmiri-cuisine/2618676)); the Budgam district administration has them "braised with ground spices and milk" ([District Budgam](https://budgam.nic.in/culinary-delight/wazwan/)).

**Yogurt, say others still.** *The Hindu*'s 2012 account, as Wikipedia's Wazwan article quotes it, has "ribs of lamb simmered in yogurt till tender, then fried" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan)), and an IANS feature reverses the order altogether: "glossy ribs cooked in ghee then simmered in yogurt till dry" ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)).

The 1988 cookbook splits the difference, simmering the ribs in water with half a teacup of milk ([Kaul Kilam](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/44.html)). And the chef's notes offer the one explanation on record for the split: "pundits boil the ribs in milk, whereas stock is preferred by Muslims" ([Chef Jolly](https://www.chefjolly.com/culinary-expedition-to-kashmir/)). That is one cook's observation rather than a documented rule, but it fits the pattern: the wazas' published method uses water; the milk versions come from general descriptions of "Kashmiri" cooking. Our own [recipe](/dishes/tabak-maaz) uses milk.

What goes into the pot is less disputed. The ribs are the point: "It is important to use the chest for the required softness and taste," Bashir Ahmad Choncha of Choncha Foods told *Deccan Chronicle* ([Deccan Chronicle](https://www.deccanchronicle.com/lifestyle/food-and-recipes/020616/wazwan-from-the-valley.html)), and in the wazas' division of a carcass the ribs go to tabak maaz while the back leg goes to gushtaba and the bony pieces to rogan josh ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/inalienable-ingredient-1499/)). The cookbook wants pieces of roughly two inches by four with the fat and skin left on, two or three bones to a piece, 25 to 30 pieces from a kilo ([Kaul Kilam](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/44.html)); at a working-class wedding in Srinagar a reporter watched the ribs being "hacked into shape" ([Outlook Traveller](https://www.outlookindia.com/traveller/ot-getaway-guides/kashmirs-legendary-wazwan-cuisine)).

## The second cooking: ghee

The frying is what makes the dish. The Waza family's recipe calls for 475 g of pure ghee to a kilo of ribs. The boiled pieces are laid in a large pan without overlapping, the ghee is poured over them, and they are fried, turning occasionally, "until they are reddish brown all over"; the ghee is drained off before serving ([Scroll, from *Wazwaan*](https://scroll.in/food/945552/tabakh-maaz)). Choncha calls it "deep fried in ghee" and "a very rich dish" ([Deccan Chronicle](https://www.deccanchronicle.com/lifestyle/food-and-recipes/020616/wazwan-from-the-valley.html)); the Gulmarg waza's version was shallow-fried "bone side down, using a weight on top to press it" ([Chef Jolly](https://www.chefjolly.com/culinary-expedition-to-kashmir/)). Sahapedia describes the result as crisp and caramelised on the outside ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)); Outlook Traveller's glossary has "unspiced rib cuts fried to a crisp" ([Outlook Traveller](https://www.outlookindia.com/traveller/ot-getaway-guides/kashmirs-legendary-wazwan-cuisine)).

That crispness is the dish's whole argument. The fat between the rib bones renders in the first cooking and sets in the second, so the piece comes to the trami glossy and dry, to be eaten with the fingers off the rice before the gravies arrive. It was the favourite dish of a Polish bride who held her wedding reception in Srinagar: "Tabakhmaaz is my favorite," she told *The Wire*, which describes it simply as "pieces of lamb ribs fried in butter" ([The Wire](https://m.thewire.in/article/travel/kashmir-wedding-wazwaan-polish-couple)).

The ghee is a puzzle of its own, because the Valley did not use it. Lawrence found in the 1890s that "clarified butter (ghi) is not much eaten", since Kashmiris believed it irritated the throat; in another passage he records that they avoided ghi, calling it the butter of India, and cooked instead with large quantities of vegetable oil ([Lawrence, p. 254](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). Yet the Valley made a great deal of it. Lawrence lists ghi as by far the most important of Kashmir's exports to the Punjab, made mostly from buffalo milk by the Gujar herders and sold through Punjabi middlemen, who adulterated it with walnut oil; cow's-milk ghi, he noted, was reckoned the most delicate, buffalo ghi next, goat's rank ([Lawrence, chapter on exports and the pages on cattle](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). Grierson's dictionary has the word, *gĕv*, clarified butter, made from butter boiled gently, strained and allowed to cool, and it records at least one meat dish cooked in it, a kabab, under *shömi* ([Grierson, pp. 316 and 882](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=butter&searchhws=no)). So the ghee of tabak maaz was, a century ago, a product the Valley exported rather than ate, and a wedding that fried its ribs in it was spending something. The pattern holds today in a smaller way: a Kashmiri restaurateur told Asia Media Centre that ghee is used for an authentic Wazwan and mustard oil at home ([Asia Media Centre](https://www.asiamediacentre.org.nz/features/the-intricacies-of-kashmiri-wazwan)).

The doctors who weighed a wedding feast in 2012 found the cost in calories. Their tabakmaaz, cooked in ghee, carried 1,470 calories per trami, or 367 for each of the four diners, more than the rogan josh (331) and the aab gosh (234), and less only than the rista, the gushtaba, the kebabs and the whole chicken, which was also fried in ghee ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)). A 2026 public-health advisory in Srinagar named "Tabak Maaz" with the meatballs and kebabs among the dishes that account for most of a trami's 6,000 to 9,000 calories, and advised moderation rather than abstinence: "Wazwan is a celebration, not an everyday meal" ([Kashmir Reader](https://kashmirreader.com/2026/06/19/choose-moderation-in-wazwan-as-single-trami-may-contain-6000-9000-calories-health-advisory/)). Two pieces of rib, eaten in the first ten minutes, are a quarter of a day's energy.

## Tabak maaz and kabargah

Kashmiri Pandits commonly call the dish kabargah ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Kashmiri_dishes)), and J&K Tourism lists kabargah among the delicacies of Kashmiri Pandit cooking ([JKTDC](https://www.jktdc.co.in/cuisines-of-kashmir.aspx)). Are they one dish or two? The sources answer both ways.

One dish, says Beg: tabakmaz in the Wazwan and kabargah on the Pandit table are "soft ribs from mutton", and "the method of preparation remains exactly the same for this dish" ([Daily Excelsior](https://www.dailyexcelsior.com/cuisines-of-kashmir-a-tradition-and-a-treasure-trove/)). Two methods, says Wikipedia's list: kabargah is "simmered on a low heat for longer and then fried very quickly", while tabak maaz is boiled with salt and garlic and has a slightly elastic texture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Kashmiri_dishes)). In Pandit homes that avoid onion and garlic, the garlic of the wazas' first boil is left out ([JKTDC](https://www.jktdc.co.in/cuisines-of-kashmir.aspx)).

The 1988 cookbook, written from the Pandit kitchen, treats the names as two finishes of one simmered rib. Its ribs are simmered once, in water and a little milk with chilli, ginger, aniseed, turmeric, asafoetida, garam masala, cloves and salt, until tender and the liquid absorbed. Then they part ways. For kabargah, each piece is dipped in a batter of curd beaten with chilli powder, garam masala and salt, and deep-fried slowly in ghee until brown. For tabaq maz, the battered pieces are sealed between the clay plates and cooked over charcoal. The book adds a detail that turns the Wazwan order upside down: in Pandit homes the dish is "generally served as a last Meat Course" ([Kaul Kilam](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/44.html)). The same ribs open a Wazwan and close a Pandit feast. [Kabargah](/dishes/kabargah) has its own page, with a Pandit recipe, and [Kashmiri Pandit food](/blog/kashmiri-pandit-food) covers that table more widely.

Lawrence recorded one thing about Pandit meat-eating that bears on the ribs. The Hindus of Kashmir, he wrote, were flesh-eaters who nevertheless avoided certain vegetables for their colour: "Tomatoes, the red-fleshed kabuli vegetable marrow, carrots, and red beans are an abomination to the Hindu" ([Lawrence, p. 300](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). A rib fried brown in ghee offended no such rule, which may be one reason the dish could cross between the two kitchens with its method, as Beg says, "exactly the same". That is our inference, not Lawrence's.

The headnote to the Waza family's cookbook draws the two traditions together rather than apart: it contrasts the Muslim wazwaan with the Pandits' *butta*, and says both favour lamb and cooking over wood fire ([Scroll, from *Wazwaan*](https://scroll.in/food/945552/tabakh-maaz)).

## First on the trami

Tabak maaz does not come in a course; it is on the platter when the platter arrives. Wikipedia and the Budgam district administration list the trami's contents as rice quartered by two seekh kebabs, four pieces of methi maaz, two of tabak maaz, and two chickens, one white and one coloured with saffron ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan); [District Budgam](https://budgam.nic.in/culinary-delight/wazwan/)). The doctors' 2012 list, "in the order in which they are served", runs methi, chicken, kabab, tabakmaaz, then dani, the single piece of meat on the bone, before rista opens the gravies ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)). Every reported feast agrees on the company it keeps: seekh kebabs, methi maaz and chicken ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258); [Asia Media Centre](https://www.asiamediacentre.org.nz/features/the-intricacies-of-kashmiri-wazwan); [Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html); [JK Arts Foundation](https://www.jkartsfoundation.in/post/kashmiri-wazwan-celebration-of-food-culture-and-people)), with a large piece of mutton, the daeni phoul, in some accounts ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)). Then the wazas start bringing the gravies, four pieces to a trami, and the long middle of the meal begins ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710)). For the order from there to gushtaba, see [how Wazwan is served](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/how-wazwan-is-served).

Its position explains its form. The first dishes are eaten fast, by hand, straight off the rice, before anything with a gravy has been served; a piece of fried rib with its fat set hard is built for exactly that.

## The feast it opens, then and now

The feast tabak maaz opens is older than any description of the dish. Lawrence's account of a Muslim wedding in the 1890s is short: "the gorgeous groom sits on cushions and the feast commences, winding up with tea"; "a big feast is given on the marriage night", the *tehl*, and the guests leave next day; the groom's father paid the bride's a *lagan chir* of twenty to fifty rupees ([Lawrence, pp. 268 to 269](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). Of the cultivators he wrote that they were "frugal in their expenditure and do not lavish money on marriage feasts or extravagances of a similar nature, as is the case in India", though he thought that might change ([Lawrence, p. 397](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). Grierson's dictionary, compiled in the same decades, has the feast's vocabulary ready: *yēra*, "a marriage feast"; *dah*, "materials (flesh, spices, etc.) for a great feast"; *wura-bal*, "the place for the cooking-fires of a bridegroom's party"; *dēg*, "a large pile of rice cooked in a cauldron, for a wedding feast or the like"; and *wāza*, "a cook-person, one who is a cook by profession, and comes of a line, or caste, of cooks" ([Grierson, pp. 1202, 199, 1138, 194 and 1178](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=feast&searchhws=no)).

It has changed, and the governments of the day have tried to change it faster. A 1974 order limiting hosts to five dishes was set aside by a sessions judge who thought it would only waste meat; a February 2017 order capped weddings at seven meat dishes, seven vegetarian dishes and two sweets ([Anadolu Agency](https://aa.com.tr/en/asia-pacific/winning-hearts-and-minds-through-famed-kashmiri-cuisine/2618676); [Gulf News/IANS](https://gulfnews.com/world/asia/india/curbs-on-guests-dishes-at-kashmir-weddings-1.1982267)). Ghulam Nabi Baba, who has cooked weddings for nearly sixty years, told Reuters that "there were only seven mutton dishes at that time. Over the centuries we added more, and now there are around 20 dishes" ([Reuters](https://kfgo.com/?p=1357989)). Through every count, the ribs have stayed on the rice at the start. Our [gushtaba story](/blog/gushtaba-last-dish-of-wazwan) tells what happens at the other end.

## Eating it for the first time

Most visitors meet tabak maaz in a Srinagar restaurant, where it is served as a starter or as one plate among several; our [guide to eating in Srinagar](/restaurants/best-wazwan-srinagar) lists places that serve Wazwan, and the [cost guide](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/cost-guide) covers prices. Expect pieces of rib that are crisp and brown at the edges and soft between the bones, with the fat left on, no gravy, and little spice; it is eaten with the fingers, with rice, and it comes first. If a menu lists kabargah instead, you now know what the difference is, and isn't.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is tabak maaz?

Lamb ribs cooked twice: simmered with spices until the bones loosen, then fried in ghee until reddish brown. It is served dry, on the rice, as one of the first dishes of a Wazwan, and is one of the seven dishes counted as essential to the feast.

### What does tabak maaz mean?

Plate-meat. In Kashmiri, *tabakh* is a large dish, plate or tray and *māz* is meat, according to Grierson's dictionary. Which plate it refers to isn't recorded: a copper tabak served food under a cover at 16th-century Kashmiri feasts, and a 1988 cookbook cooks its tabaq maz sealed between clay plates called tabaqs.

### How is tabak maaz cooked?

The Waza family's published recipe boils the whole rib cage in water with garlic and salt until the membrane can be pierced with a thumb, cuts it into eight pieces, boils them again with turmeric, dry ginger, cloves and black cardamom, and fries them in pure ghee until reddish brown all over.

### Is tabak maaz cooked in milk or water?

Sources disagree. The wazas' own recipe and a Srinagar waza use water; Wikipedia's article on the dish, a news agency and the Budgam district page say milk; *The Hindu* and an IANS feature say yogurt. One chef who cooked with a Gulmarg waza reports that Pandits boil the ribs in milk while Muslims prefer stock.

### Why is tabak maaz fried in ghee?

The frying sets the fat between the bones and gives the dish its crisp, glossy, dry finish, which suits a piece eaten by hand off the rice before any gravy arrives. The ghee is a feast ingredient: in the 1890s Walter Lawrence found that Kashmiris cooked with oil and exported their ghi, and a restaurateur today says ghee is for an authentic Wazwan and mustard oil for home.

### How many calories are in tabak maaz?

At the Srinagar wedding studied by SKIMS doctors in 2012, the tabak maaz carried about 1,470 calories per trami, or 367 per diner. A 2026 health advisory named it among the dishes that make up most of a trami's estimated 6,000 to 9,000 calories.

### Where does the mutton for tabak maaz come from?

Mostly from outside Kashmir. Official figures reported between 2012 and 2026 put imports at 55 to 70 per cent of the Valley's mutton, trucked up the Jammu-Srinagar highway from Rajasthan, Haryana, Gujarat and Uttar Pradesh; Kashmir's own flocks, now largely Kashmir Merino crossbreds, supply the rest.

### What is the difference between tabak maaz and kabargah?

Kabargah is the Kashmiri Pandit name for the same ribs. One writer says the method is exactly the same; Wikipedia's list of Kashmiri dishes says kabargah is simmered longer and fried faster; a Pandit cookbook dips the ribs in a curd batter before frying and leaves out garlic. In Pandit homes it is often the last meat course, where in a Wazwan it is among the first.

### When is tabak maaz served at a Wazwan?

At the very start. It is on the trami when it arrives, with the rice, seekh kebabs, methi maaz and chicken, before any of the gravies are served.

### Does tabak maaz have a GI tag?

Not yet. An application for "Tabakmaaz (Meat Products) of Kashmir" was filed on 8 August 2024 and was still at pre-examination when we checked the register on 15 October 2026.

## Fact-check list (for editors; not for publication)

Every claim is listed with its source in `../research/03-tabak-maaz.md`. Points to weigh:

1. **"Plate-meat" and the two plate threads.** Grierson's entries are read. The link between the dish's name and either the copper tabak or the clay tabaqs is presented as two possibilities that "fit the word", not as a finding. Keep it that way. The clay-versus-copper paragraph adds Lawrence's earthenware platters and Grierson's "used only by Musalmāns" note as context, not as an answer.
2. **The mutton figures** come from five different years and sources (2012 legislative council figures via *Kashmir Life*; 2014 *Kashmir Life* editorial; 2019 sheep husbandry director via Village Square; 2021 *Greater Kashmir*; 2026 *Kashmir Times*) and do not reconcile; the article says so and draws only the shape (roughly half imported). Do not average them.
3. **Kashmir Merino history.** The breeding sequence (Australian Merino rams, Delaine rams, F2 selection) is from Athar Parvaiz's two 2019 reports quoting the sheep husbandry director; "from the 1960s" is the director's "since we started", which the articles do not date precisely. A 2022 review cited in the ledger names Rambouillet rather than Delaine in the exotic ancestry (from a 2004 source). The article gives the director's version and does not state a start date beyond "from the 1960s"; an editor may prefer "in the second half of the twentieth century".
4. **Sahapedia's spice list** for the first simmer (cardamom, cinnamon, ginger powder, garlic paste, cloves) is as recorded in the site's dish notes from a 12 October 2026 reading; this session's reading of Sahapedia confirmed the simmer-then-fry method and "a starter" but did not re-list the spices.
5. **Chef Jolly** is a personal blog; the article attributes its "pundits boil in milk" remark explicitly as one cook's observation.
6. **Beg's Kazakhstan remark** is quoted as an unsupported claim; an editor may prefer to cut it.
7. **"1988"** for *Culinary Art of Kashmir* comes from Wikipedia's reference list; the online edition is dated 2005.
8. **The GI status** was checked on 15 October 2026; re-check on publication if later.
9. **Lawrence's page numbers** are the printed pages as read in the Internet Archive OCR text. The ghi-export and cattle passages were found through the archive's full-text search (scan pages 397 to 398 and 431 to 434), whose snippets do not show printed page numbers, so the article cites them by chapter. The "rough earthenware" sentence was likewise found by search (scan page 78) and is cited as "elsewhere in the book".
10. The sentences "Its position explains its form", "the dish's whole argument", "a dish named for a plate had both to choose from", and the inference about Hindu colour rules and the brown rib are editorial observations drawn from the sourced facts around them, and are marked as such where they could be mistaken for findings.
