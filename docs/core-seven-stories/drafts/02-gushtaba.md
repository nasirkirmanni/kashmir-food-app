The sound comes before the dish. As the wedding season opens in autumn, Reuters reported in 2026, "the rhythmic thud of wooden mallets pounding lamb" carries through the courtyards and lanes of Kashmir ([Reuters via Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/gallery/2026/9/30/photos-kashmirs-600-year-old-wazwan-wedding-feast-adapts-to-modern-life)). At one Srinagar wedding a reporter counted eight men at it, each with a stone mortar and a heavy wooden mallet, each pounding one spice into the meat before passing it on; the last man pounded in the lamb fat, "turning the mash pink", and what came off the stones were "glistening oval discs" ([Outlook Traveller](https://www.outlookindia.com/traveller/ot-getaway-guides/kashmirs-legendary-wazwan-cuisine)). Hours later, in a white gravy of yogurt and broth, those discs will have swollen into the dish that tells four hundred guests the meal is over.

Gushtaba is the last dish of a Wazwan, and the one by which the cook is judged. "As a rule, the meal must end with gushtaba," a Kashmiri restaurateur told Asia Media Centre ([Asia Media Centre](https://www.asiamediacentre.org.nz/features/the-intricacies-of-kashmiri-wazwan)). Every account of the feast agrees on that. This is the story of the dish behind the rule: what its name means, what the oldest Kashmiri dictionary knew about the cooks who make it, where it may have come from, why the meat has to be pounded within an hour of slaughter, what the food scientists of Srinagar found when they put it on the bench, what goes into its gravy, what it weighs and what it costs the body, how it is shared, and what it has come to stand for. For what the dish is, with a recipe and where to eat it, see [gushtaba](/dishes/gushtaba); for how it compares with its red twin, see [rista vs gushtaba](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/rista-vs-gushtaba).

## What gushtaba is

Gushtaba is a large ball of mutton pounded with fat, boiled, and then cooked in yakhni, a white gravy of yogurt and mutton broth ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)). Wikipedia calls it "a velvety textured meatball in white yogurt gravy" and counts it among the seven dishes that are "a must" at a Wazwan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan)); the Waza family's cookbook lists it among the same seven ([Roli Books](https://rolibooks.com/product/wazwaan-traditional-kashmiri-cuisine/)), and a 2016 review of the feast's meat products names it with kabab, tabak maaz, aab gosh, rogan josh, yakhni and rista ([Rather, Masoodi and Akhter](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jef.2016.12.002)). A second paper by the same Kashmir University group, published a year earlier, puts it even more plainly: "Goshtaba forms the essential item of Kashmiri wazwan" ([Rather and others, *Journal of Food Science and Technology*, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4648903/)).

It is big. Sahapedia records a single gushtaba of 200 to 400 g that serves the four diners at a trami, and a waza in downtown Srinagar describes one large meatball cut into four ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy); [The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)). Doctors who weighed the dishes at a Srinagar wedding in 2012 recorded a "500 - 750 gms mutton ball" ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi, SKIMS, 2012](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)). Where each diner gets a rista of their own, the gushtaba is shared.

## The name, and the titles

The dictionary of the Kashmiri language has the word. Under *gōshĕth*, "meat, flesh", Grierson's *Dictionary of the Kashmiri Language* enters *gusht-āba*: "a rich soup of pounded or minced meat" ([Grierson, p. 309](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=gosht&searchhws=no)). The first half is *gosht*, meat. If the second is the Persian *āb*, water, which is the same element as in the Persian dish abgoosht, "water" plus "meat" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abgoosht)), then gushtaba is simply meat-broth: the dictionary's "rich soup", named for its gravy rather than its ball. The name is Persian in origin, like those of rogan josh, yakhni, aab gosht and rista ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine), citing the 2016 review). It is spelled many ways: Wikipedia's article is titled "Goshtaab", a 1988 Kashmiri cookbook writes "Goashtaba", the Srinagar doctors wrote "Goshtaba", and the application now before India's Geographical Indications Registry is for "Goshtaba (Meat Products) of Kashmir" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goshtaab); [Kaul Kilam](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/205.html); [GI Registry, application 1337](https://search.ipindia.gov.in/GIRPublicSearch/Application/Details/1337)).

The titles are grander than the name. The 1988 cookbook says Muslims call it "The King of Dishes" or the "Dish of Kings" ([Kaul Kilam](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/205.html)). Wikipedia calls it the "king of Kashmiri wazwan" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goshtaab)). "It's considered the king of all Kashmiri dishes," Bashir Ahmad Choncha, who runs Choncha Foods in Srinagar, told *Deccan Chronicle* ([Deccan Chronicle](https://www.deccanchronicle.com/lifestyle/food-and-recipes/020616/wazwan-from-the-valley.html)). *Kashmir Life* calls it "the finest delicacy of Wazwaan" and "the most eagerly awaited dish served at the end" ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/kashmir-wazwan-issue-42-vol-12-257951/); [Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/inalienable-ingredient-1499/)). To the waza Mohammad Shafi Khan it is "the showstopper" ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)); to a Gulf News writer at his cousin's wedding, "a yardstick to measure the chef's culinary skills" that "serves as a full stop" ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710)).

## The cook, the kitchen and the feast, in the dictionary

Grierson's dictionary was compiled from the 1890s and published between 1916 and 1932, and it knew the trade that makes gushtaba better than it knew the dish. Its entry for *wāza* is "a cook-person, one who is a cook by profession, and comes of a line, or caste, of cooks", and *wāzagī* is the profession itself ([Grierson, pp. 1178 to 1179](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=cook&searchhws=no)). Two other words, *nözir* and *sŏlhöri*, are glossed as a head cook, the figure a wedding kitchen now calls the vasta waza ([Grierson, pp. 676 and 909](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=cook&searchhws=no)). Under *kömü* the dictionary records a proverb that asks "what have cooks to do with kings" ([Grierson, p. 444](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=cook&searchhws=no)); the dish the cooks are judged by would, in time, be called the Dish of Kings.

The feast is there too, word by word. *Wura* is a kitchen, and the dictionary notes that it is usually set up temporarily on the occasion of feasts; *wura-bal* is "the place for the cooking-fires of a bridegroom's party" ([Grierson, p. 1138](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=kitchen&searchhws=no)). *Dēg* is "a large pile of rice cooked in a cauldron, for a wedding feast or the like" ([Grierson, p. 194](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=cauldron&searchhws=no)), which is the word the wazas still use for their copper pots. *Dah* is "materials (flesh, spices, etc.) for a great feast" ([Grierson, p. 199](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=feast&searchhws=no)). *Yēra* is "a marriage feast" ([Grierson, p. 1202](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=feast&searchhws=no)). *Trömi* is "a copper dish (used only by Musalmāns) out of which food is eaten", and *trāmi-bata* is the food served in those copper vessels to the Muslim guests at a wedding feast ([Grierson, p. 1026](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=copper&searchhws=no)). *Pötar* is "eating from the same dish or at the same feast" ([Grierson, p. 804](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=feast&searchhws=no)), which is what four people at a trami do. And *māzarath* is "hospitable pressing to eat (at a feast)" ([Grierson, p. 616](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=feast&searchhws=no)): the custom, described below, by which the gushtaba is never refused.

One more entry belongs here. *Zang* is an omen of good luck, and the dictionary's example is "a dish of curds" placed on the right hand before starting an undertaking ([Grierson, p. 1227](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=curds&searchhws=no)). Curds were auspicious in the Valley long before anyone wrote down that the feast should end in a dish of them. Whether that is why the last dish is white, nobody has recorded; the dictionary gives the custom and the dish, not the link between them.

## Where it may have come from

Less is documented about gushtaba's past than about its present. Wikipedia says the dish "is believed to have been introduced" during the reign of the Mughal emperors, as part of the Central Asian, Persian and Mughal influence on Kashmiri cooking, citing the food historian Colleen Taylor Sen ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goshtaab)). The 2016 review's authors are blunter about the state of the record: the feast's "traditional knowledge is poorly documented" ([Rather, Masoodi and Akhter](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jef.2016.12.002)), and no document records when or how Wazwan began at all ([JK Arts Foundation](https://www.jkartsfoundation.in/post/kashmiri-wazwan-celebration-of-food-culture-and-people)). The wazas themselves keep a count rather than a date: "There were only seven mutton dishes at that time. Over the centuries we added more, and now there are around 20 dishes," Ghulam Nabi Baba, 74, whose forefathers settled in Srinagar's old city in the cooks' quarter of Wazpora, told Reuters in 2026 ([Reuters](https://kfgo.com/?p=1357989)).

Two older threads are worth following. The first is the technique. Pounded meat shaped into balls has a Persian name of its own, kofta, a loanword from the Persian *koofta*, "pounded meat"; the earliest known recipes for it are in early Arab cookbooks, for "large ground lamb meatballs triple-glazed in a mixture of saffron and egg yolk", and a stuffed version, nargisi kofta, was served at the Mughal court ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kofta)). When a former head of Kashmir University's Persian department, Shams-ud-din Ahmad, listed the mutton dishes that Persian cooks taught Kashmiris, he named kofta and rista among them, with kabab, rogan josh, korma, yakhni, pulao and aab gosht ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338), which summarises his book *Shah-i-Hamdan aur Kashmir*). Gushtaba is not on that list by name, but its two halves are: the pounded ball and the curd gravy.

The second is the gravy. The earliest description of Kashmiri feasting that *Kashmir Life* could find, in the *Tuhfat-ul-Ahbab*, a Persian work completed in the early 17th century, has 16th-century nobles and Sufi lodges serving kabab, pulao and yakhni, "meat cooked in curd" ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/kashmir-wazwan-issue-42-vol-12-257951/)). Grierson's dictionary defines *yĕkhañĕ* the same way: "a kind of dish (consisting of meat cooked with spices in curdled milk)" ([Grierson, p. 1192](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/grierson_query.py?qs=milk&searchhws=no)). Whether a pounded ball was cooked in that curd four hundred years ago, nobody has recorded. Our [history of Wazwan](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/history-of-wazwan) sets out the rest of what is known, and what is only told.

## The feast in 1895

The fullest outside description of Kashmiri eating before the twentieth century is Walter Lawrence's, in *The Valley of Kashmir* (1895), written after years as the state's settlement commissioner. He does not name gushtaba, or any Wazwan dish. What he describes is the gap between everyday food and the food of a feast. The ordinary meal, he wrote, showed little taste; but when "the professional cook is called in some taste is displayed in the preparation and arrangement of viands", and "the earthenware platters filled with brightly-coloured foods make a pretty show" ([Lawrence, p. 253](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). The ordinary cultivator ate fowl about six times a month and mutton "perhaps five times in the month"; clarified butter was "not much eaten", because Kashmiris believed it irritated the throat; and, in a judgement the feast has done nothing to soften, "the Kashmiri Musalman is extravagant and greedy" ([Lawrence, p. 254](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)).

His account of a Muslim wedding is brief. "The gorgeous groom sits on cushions and the feast commences, winding up with tea"; "a big feast is given on the marriage night", the *tehl*, and the next day the guests depart ([Lawrence, p. 269](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). Of the agricultural classes he wrote that they were "frugal in their expenditure and do not lavish money on marriage feasts or extravagances of a similar nature, as is the case in India" ([Lawrence, p. 397](https://archive.org/details/valleyofkashmir00lawr)). Two things in those pages matter for this story. The professional cook was already a figure in the Valley's kitchens in the 1890s, called in for occasions. And the platters he filled were earthenware: the copper trami that Grierson's dictionary reserves for Muslim feasts does not appear in Lawrence's description of the groom's dinner. Whether that reflects what Lawrence saw or what he chose to write down, we cannot tell.

## The meat: why it is pounded within the hour

Gushtaba is made from a sheep that was alive that morning. The meat is minced "directly after slaughter", Sahapedia says, with a wooden hammer on a flat stone, and fresh sheep fat and black cardamom are pounded in ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)). At a wedding the meat is prepared for cooking within one hour of butchering ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)). Shafi Khan, a second-generation waza, puts the rule plainly: "If the mutton is left idle and minced later, one can't make goshtab" ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)). Wikipedia's account, drawn from *The Hindu*, is that the sheep must be slaughtered fresh and the meat pounded before rigor mortis sets in, and that the two meatball dishes are rarely found outside the Valley because the texture is so hard to achieve ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)).

The cut is the back leg, which goes into gushtaba and kebabs while the ribs go to tabak maaz and the bony pieces to rogan josh and korma ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/inalienable-ingredient-1499/)). The 1988 cookbook agrees: boneless leg, "preferably freshly slaughtered", with about 250 g of suet to the kilo, softened in hot water ([Kaul Kilam](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/190.html)).

Then the pounding. The tool has three names in our sources. The cookbook calls the wooden mallet a *goshpar*; the Srinagar doctors who watched a wedding kitchen in 2012 wrote that the meat "is minced on stony surfaces with a tukni (a wooden instrument)"; Reuters simply says wooden mallets on stone slabs ([Kaul Kilam](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/190.html); [Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153); [Reuters via Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/gallery/2026/9/30/photos-kashmirs-600-year-old-wazwan-wedding-feast-adapts-to-modern-life)). The meat is cubed on a block, pounded a few pieces at a time, and the warm suet is pounded in as it goes ([Kaul Kilam](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/190.html)). At weddings the mallets are walnut wood and the work is done by "a line of chefs" on stone slabs ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710); [Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/inalienable-ingredient-1499/)). The meat is beaten until it "loses its stringiness" ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)), until, as one account has it, "the nerves dissolve" ([JK Arts Foundation](https://www.jkartsfoundation.in/post/kashmiri-wazwan-celebration-of-food-culture-and-people)). It is folded as it is beaten, which works air in and keeps the finished ball light ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmiri_cuisine)). "We pound the meat by hand for a very long time," says Choncha ([Deccan Chronicle](https://www.deccanchronicle.com/lifestyle/food-and-recipes/020616/wazwan-from-the-valley.html)). The fat is not a garnish but the structure: the finished ball arrives, in the Gulf News writer's words, "marinated with blobs of golden fat" ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710)), and when the discs went into the gravy at the wedding Outlook Traveller watched, "the melting fat stretched the discs into enormous orbs" ([Outlook Traveller](https://www.outlookindia.com/traveller/ot-getaway-guides/kashmirs-legendary-wazwan-cuisine)).

The wazas do not explain the hour's deadline in the language of science, but meat science explains it for them. As rigor mortis sets in after slaughter, the muscle's pH falls, the acid cancels the charges on the muscle proteins, and the proteins actin and myosin lock into actomyosin, which "physically reduces the space between protein chains": the meat holds less water. Before rigor, muscle is "the easiest form from which to extract the highly functional protein, myosin", and mixing salt into it at that stage preserves that state; products made this way have "a firmer texture ... with less cooking loss". Those are the findings of an Ohio State University meat scientist writing about sausage, where the window for salting pork is 90 minutes from bleeding ([Lynn Knipe, Ohio State University](https://meatsci.osu.edu/node/127)). A 2021 review in *Foods* describes pre-rigor muscle as having "excellent processing characteristics with high pH and relaxed microstructure", and salt as the agent that improves its protein solubility, emulsifying capacity and water-holding capacity ([Kim and others, *Foods*, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8145339/)). None of this research is about gushtaba. But fresh-killed meat, pounded with fat and salt within the hour, is exactly the process it describes, and the spring and lightness that Kashmiris prize in the ball is what it predicts.

Home cooks who cannot buy a sheep that morning reach for binders. The 1988 cookbook kneads in arrowroot and allows a food processor for small batches ([Kaul Kilam](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/190.html)); a Kashmiri home cook in Dubai who published a goshtaba recipe in Gulf News mixes egg whites and mustard oil into the mince ([Abdul Rashid Waza, Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/cooking-cuisines/classic-kashmiri-goshtaba-recipe-from-a-waza-1.1642654487264)). Wazas use none of these; they have the stone, the mallet and the hour.

## What the laboratory made of it

Gushtaba has a small scientific literature of its own, almost all of it from Srinagar, and it confirms the wazas on every point they care about. The earliest work we have found is a 1988 veterinary thesis at the Indian Veterinary Research Institute, A. H. Samoon's "Processing and preservation of Goshtaba and Rista (Kashmiri meat products)", which later papers still cite as the standard method ([Jalal and others, *Indian Journal of Animal Sciences*, 2015](https://epubs.icar.org.in/index.php/IJAnS/article/download/52460/22155/380222)). Samoon's published study of rista, in *Meat Science* in 1994, tested the two things the wazas insist on, fresh meat and fat: "Incorporation of lamb fat at 20% level was found optimum", and both pre-rigor and chilled meat were used, with the hand-minced, hot-processed product binding better than machine-minced meat from chilled carcasses ([Samoon and Sharma, *Meat Science*, 1994](https://doi.org/10.1016/0309-1740(94)90052-3)).

The Kashmir University food technologists who took up the work in the 2010s describe gushtaba in the vocabulary of their trade: "an emulsion type ground meat product", in which "considerable amount of animal fat (20 %) is used in its formulation to achieve a stable emulsion" ([Rather and others, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4648903/)). Their laboratory version is instructive for what it keeps and what it shrinks. The meat is "fresh mutton from hind leg portion", the wazas' cut; the fat is adjusted to 20 per cent with mutton fat; the seasoning is 2.5 per cent salt, large cardamom and cumin; and the yakhni is "two parts of fresh curd" to one of water, with cardamom, cinnamon, cloves, ginger, aniseed, garlic, fried onion and salt, brought to the boil before the balls go in for 30 minutes. But the balls are "compact balls of 60 mm diameter and 50–60 g weight", a tenth of the wedding ball. The finished control product was 64 per cent water, 15 per cent protein and 17 per cent fat, with a pH of 5.3, and the tasting panel gave it 7.3 out of 9 ([Rather and others, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4648903/)).

A veterinary group at the Sher-e-Kashmir agricultural university worked the same way, and their paper records the wazas' deadline in laboratory terms: the mutton and fat were "used for the preparation of the products within 2 h of post-mortem". Their formulation was 90 per cent boneless mutton to 10 per cent fat, with 2.5 per cent salt, 10 per cent chilled water and 0.2 per cent large cardamom; they note that traditional goshtaba "with an average fat content of 15–25% is having a high sensory appeal", and they found that even their low-fat version kept for 20 days in a refrigerator ([Jalal and others, 2015](https://epubs.icar.org.in/index.php/IJAnS/article/download/52460/22155/380222)). The whole research programme, from xanthan gum and apple pomace to retort pouches, is an attempt to make a gushtaba that travels; none of the papers claims to have matched the wedding kitchen's ball. For the dish's texture the science has one consistent explanation, fresh meat and fat, and the wazas have had it for generations.

## The yakhni

The balls are boiled first, in water until they are soft and have expanded ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)), or, in the cookbook's method, for about half an hour with salt, aniseed and ginger powder, after which the broth is strained and kept ([Kaul Kilam](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/205.html)). Then they go into the yakhni.

Sahapedia describes the wazas' yakhni as yogurt and mutton broth with shallots, ginger, garlic, cinnamon and cardamom, topped with cumin and dry mint, "a creamy white soup" ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)). Shafi Khan's has yogurt, ghee and mutton stock with mild spices and dry mint ([The Kashmir Walla](https://thekashmirwalla.com/?p=72338)); a news-agency account names curd, cardamom, dry ginger and fennel ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)). What it never has is chilli or turmeric: yakhni is the gravy made "without turmeric or chilli powder" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Kashmiri_dishes)), which is why gushtaba is white where rista is red. The cookbook's method shows the one technical danger: the curd and broth are cooked with cloves and garlic, "stirred constantly so the curd doesn't split", with fried shallot and onion, over a low heat until the oil and ghee rise, and finished with garam masala, cinnamon, cardamoms and mint ([Kaul Kilam](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/205.html)). The former tourism director M. Saleem Beg compresses the whole dish into a phrase: "pounded mutton balls cooked in preheated curd" ([Daily Excelsior](https://www.dailyexcelsior.com/cuisines-of-kashmir-a-tradition-and-a-treasure-trove/)).

The dried mint on top is the dish's signature scent, and the cumin its warmth; the gravy's heat, such as it is, comes from the spices, not from chilli. Wikipedia's Wazwan article calls the gravy "spicy" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan)); every detailed account lists cardamom, cinnamon, ginger, fennel, cloves and mint, and none lists chilli. For the spices themselves, see [fennel and dry ginger](/blog/fennel-and-dry-ginger-wazwan).

## The last dish

Every source has gushtaba at the end. Wikipedia: the main course "usually ends with gushtaba" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan)). Reuters: "the final dish of a Wazwan feast, signalling the end of the meal" ([Reuters via Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/gallery/2026/9/30/photos-kashmirs-600-year-old-wazwan-wedding-feast-adapts-to-modern-life)). Beg: "the final dish announcing the completion of servings" ([Daily Excelsior](https://www.dailyexcelsior.com/cuisines-of-kashmir-a-tradition-and-a-treasure-trove/)). The 1988 cookbook: "the last Meat Dish in a 'Vazavan' Feast, before the Dessert" ([Kaul Kilam](https://ikashmir.net/cookbook/recipes/205.html)). In Outlook India's account of the order, yakhni is "the finishing dish served in wazwan before gushtaba is served" ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258)). The Srinagar doctors' list of the eleven dishes served at the wedding they studied, "in the order in which they are served", ends with goshtaba ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)). A dessert such as phirni follows, and kahwa, poured from a samovar, closes the feast ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazwan); [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/rogan-josh-tabak-maaz-rista-goshtaba-royal-kashmiri-wazwan-a-feast-for-the-senses-1.1642605703710); [Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)).

None of these sources says why. What they do describe is how the last dish is eaten, and that may be the answer. Each diner at the trami has had a rista of their own; the gushtaba comes one to a trami, and the eldest of the four divides it ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)). Guests often take fresh rice just before it arrives ([JK Arts Foundation](https://www.jkartsfoundation.in/post/kashmiri-wazwan-celebration-of-food-culture-and-people)). It is the richest thing on the table, the dish the cook is judged by, and the only one the four must share out among themselves. A feast that opens with the washing of hands and seats its guests in fours ends, in other words, with an act of division performed by the senior person at each platter. That reading is ours, not a source's; the sources give the rule and the ritual and leave the reason unsaid.

They are clear about one more thing. Kashmiri hosts press food on their guests, and it is thought disrespectful to refuse a dish or an extra helping ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/food/features/2021/11/10/wazwan-royal-meal-of-kashmir-paradise.html)). Grierson's dictionary had a word for the pressing, *māzarath*, a century ago. The gushtaba is not turned down. [How Wazwan is served](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/how-wazwan-is-served) covers the rest of the manners at the trami.

## What a gushtaba weighs, and what it costs

In the autumn of 2012 three doctors from the Sher-i-Kashmir Institute of Medical Sciences, Mushtaq Ahmad of the medicine department and Sakina Akhter and Shariq Masoodi of endocrinology, chose a wedding at random, asked the host and the vasta waza for consent, and had the waza "keep a record of every ingredient used and the number of servings prepared". They repeated the exercise three times, because the meat in a trami for four "varies greatly (usually from 3 kg to 5 kg)", and worked out the calories from the National Institute of Nutrition's tables ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi, *JMS SKIMS*, 2012](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)). Their goshtaba was a "500 - 750 gms mutton ball", "prepared in spheres then cooked in curd", one to a trami. It carried 1,787 calories, or 447 for each of the four diners; across the eleven dishes they counted, the trami held 18,484 calories before rice, and 21,933 with the one or two kilograms of rice that each trami consumed. "Presuming that one Tramie will serve four persons, the total calories consumed on an average by each person would be 5483." Their title was "Wazwan the Kashmiri Cuisine - A Caloric Bomb".

Two cautions. The paper is a two-page brief report, and it contains a slip: its text says the dishes ranged "from 709±60 Calories in 'Dani' to 3597±1348 Calories in 'Rista'", but its own table gives the 3,597 figure to the whole chicken, fried in ghee, and rista 1,814. And its introduction repeats the Timur legend as fact, citing Wikipedia. What stands is the fieldwork: a waza's own ingredient list, weighed, at a real wedding. Gushtaba was the third heaviest mutton dish on the trami after rista and the kebabs, and the authors' advice was not to abandon the feast but "to reduce the amount served per person like decreasing the portion size". They also noted that a search of PubMed for "Wazwan" in a title found nothing; theirs was the first paper ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)).

Fourteen years later a public-health advisory issued in Srinagar put the same point in gentler numbers: "a typical Wazwan trami serving four people may contain between 6,000 and 9,000 calories", or "approximately 1,500 to 2,250 calories per person", which can be "as much as 80 to 125 per cent of an average adult's daily calorie requirement in a single meal". The advisory named "meatballs such as Rista and Gushtaba" among the main contributors, and ended: "Wazwan is a celebration, not an everyday meal. Enjoy our heritage, protect your health, and choose moderation" ([Kashmir Reader](https://kashmirreader.com/2026/06/19/choose-moderation-in-wazwan-as-single-trami-may-contain-6000-9000-calories-health-advisory/)). The 2012 and 2026 figures differ by a factor of two or more; the first was measured at one wedding, the second is an estimate for a typical one, and neither pretends to be the last word.

## The dish of state

Gushtaba's titles have followed it out of the wedding tent. *Kashmir Life* prints, as folklore, the story of Bakhshi Ghulam Mohammad, who became the state's prime minister in August 1953, feeding gushtaba to the visiting Soviet leader ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/kashmir-wazwan-issue-42-vol-12-257951/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakshi_Ghulam_Mohammad)). The visit itself is a matter of record. Nikita Khrushchev and Nikolai Bulganin toured India in the autumn of 1955, and in India Khrushchev announced Soviet support for Indian sovereignty over Kashmir ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India%E2%80%93Russia_relations); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Bulganin)). Jawaharlal Nehru's letters from those weeks, published in his *Selected Works*, show the Srinagar leg being planned for 9 December: Bakhshi was to receive the two men at the airport and proposed a garden party at Shalimar and a banquet, and Nehru, accepting the garden party, wrote that he was "rather nervous of Kashmiri banquets. I fear that the food they will get there might upset them"; a week after the visit he wrote that it "had passed off so successfully" and that the Soviet leaders "spoke highly of" Bakhshi ([Nehru Archive, letters of 23 November, 2 December and 17 December 1955](https://nehruarchive.in/documents/to-bakhshi-ghulam-mohammad-23-november-1955-48e115)). Whether gushtaba was on the table that December is not in the letters. A news-agency feature goes further, saying that the famous photograph of Bakshi "thrusting goshtaba" at Khrushchev "is understood to have turned high tables of global diplomacy in India's favor"; the claim is attributed to no one, and we repeat it only as the story Kashmir tells ([Anadolu Agency](https://aa.com.tr/en/asia-pacific/winning-hearts-and-minds-through-famed-kashmiri-cuisine/2618676)). That the story is told at all says what the dish means there.

Wazwan has stayed on the diplomatic menu since, from the Pakistani delegation told in 2004 that they were in Kashmir "to taste Wazwan" to the governments that have tried to shrink the feast: a 1960s attempt to phase it out, a 1974 order limiting hosts to five dishes that a sessions judge set aside on the ground that it would only waste meat, and a February 2017 order capping weddings at seven meat dishes, seven vegetarian dishes and two sweets ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-kashmir-s-famed-wazwan-is-always-on-the-diplomacy-menu-news-193258); [Anadolu Agency](https://aa.com.tr/en/asia-pacific/winning-hearts-and-minds-through-famed-kashmiri-cuisine/2618676); [Gulf News/IANS](https://gulfnews.com/world/asia/india/curbs-on-guests-dishes-at-kashmir-weddings-1.1982267)). Through all of them, gushtaba has kept its place at the end.

## Gushtaba today

The dish is changing at the edges and not at the centre. Rista and gushtaba are now also made with chicken and fish, a Srinagar chef told *Kashmir Life* in 2011, and the same chef, Bashir Ahmad Choncha, had moved into processed Wazwan ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/inalienable-ingredient-1499/)). The Srinagar doctors noted in 2012 that "ready-made Wazwan is now available on a fly", so that Kashmiris were eating the feast "every now and then" rather than at weddings alone ([Ahmad, Akhter and Masoodi](https://www.jmsskims.org/index.php/jms/article/view/153)). The wazas' children are leaving. "Our kids didn't continue the tradition of cooking in weddings because they said it's too hectic," Ghulam Nabi Baba told Reuters; his son has opened a restaurant instead, and other young cooks have followed him ([Reuters](https://kfgo.com/?p=1357989); [Reuters via Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/gallery/2026/9/30/photos-kashmirs-600-year-old-wazwan-wedding-feast-adapts-to-modern-life)); younger men are leaving the trade ([Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/wazwan-culinary-legacy)). At the centre, the meat is still pounded by hand on stone and cooked over wood ([Reuters via Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/gallery/2026/9/30/photos-kashmirs-600-year-old-wazwan-wedding-feast-adapts-to-modern-life)).

The sheep that make it are, increasingly, not Kashmiri. "Normally, at an average marriage we cook 200 kgs of mutton," Baba says; forty years ago, at a village wedding in north Kashmir, he cooked 4,500 kg ([Reuters](https://kfgo.com/?p=1357989)). A news agency's estimate in 2026 was "around 500 kilogrammes of mutton and 150 kilogrammes of chicken" at an average wedding and more than a tonne at a lavish one ([PTI via Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/news/mutton-shortage-clouds-kashmiri-weddings-traders-blame-punjab)). Kashmir produces about half of what it eats; the rest comes up the highway in trucks from Rajasthan and beyond, and when Punjab levied a tax on those trucks in the summer of 2026 the dealers stopped importing and families began talking of postponing weddings ([PTI via Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/news/mutton-shortage-clouds-kashmiri-weddings-traders-blame-punjab)). Our [tabak maaz story](/blog/tabak-maaz-ribs-cooked-twice) follows the sheep; it is the same animal, whichever dish it ends in.

In August 2024 The Kashmir Wazwan Cooperative Limited applied to register "Goshtaba (Meat Products) of Kashmir" as a geographical indication. When we checked the register on 17 October 2026 the application was still at pre-examination ([GI Registry, application 1337](https://search.ipindia.gov.in/GIRPublicSearch/Application/Details/1337)). Our [GI guide](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/gi-tag-gushtaba-tabak-maaz) explains what registration would mean and how to check the status yourself.

## Eating it for the first time

Weddings are private, so most visitors meet gushtaba in a Srinagar restaurant; our [guide to eating in Srinagar](/restaurants/best-wazwan-srinagar) lists places that serve it, and the [cost guide](/kashmiri-food/wazwan/guide/cost-guide) covers prices. In a set Wazwan it comes last, so don't ask for it first. Expect one large ball in a white gravy, rich with fat and scented with dried mint, to be eaten with rice; on a shared trami, let the eldest divide it. It is mild. If you are offered more, the custom is to accept.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is gushtaba?

A large ball of mutton pounded with fat, boiled, and cooked in yakhni, a white gravy of yogurt and mutton broth scented with cardamom, fennel, dry ginger and dried mint. It is one of the seven essential dishes of a Wazwan and is served last.

### Why is gushtaba served last?

Every account of the feast records that it ends with gushtaba, but none explains the rule. What the sources describe is that one gushtaba serves the four diners at a trami and the eldest divides it, where each diner has a rista of their own; the shared, richest dish closes the meal, and dessert and kahwa follow.

### What does gushtaba mean?

Grierson's dictionary of Kashmiri enters *gusht-āba* under *gōshĕth*, meat, and defines it as "a rich soup of pounded or minced meat". If the second half is the Persian *āb*, water, the name means meat-broth. It is also called the Dish of Kings.

### How is gushtaba made?

From a sheep slaughtered the same morning. The leg meat is pounded with a wooden mallet on a flat stone within the hour, with fresh fat, black cardamom and salt worked in, until it is smooth and light; shaped into large balls; boiled; and then cooked in yakhni.

### How big is a gushtaba?

Sahapedia gives 200 to 400 g for the ball that serves a trami of four; doctors who weighed the dishes at a Srinagar wedding in 2012 recorded 500 to 750 g. Laboratory versions made for research are a tenth of that, about 50 to 60 g.

### How many calories are in gushtaba?

At the wedding studied by SKIMS doctors in 2012, the gushtaba carried about 1,787 calories per trami, or 447 per diner, and the whole feast about 5,483 per person including rice. A 2026 public-health advisory in Srinagar estimated 1,500 to 2,250 calories per person for a typical trami.

### What is the difference between gushtaba and rista?

Both start from the same pounded mutton. Rista is smaller, one per diner, in a red gravy coloured with Kashmiri chilli and served early; gushtaba is larger, one per trami, in a white yogurt gravy and served last. See our guide to rista vs gushtaba.

### Is gushtaba spicy?

No. Its gravy has no chilli or turmeric; its flavour comes from cardamom, cinnamon, fennel, dry ginger, cloves, cumin and dried mint.

### Does gushtaba have a GI tag?

Not yet. An application for "Goshtaba (Meat Products) of Kashmir" was filed on 8 August 2024 and was still at pre-examination when we checked the register on 17 October 2026.

### Can you refuse gushtaba at a Wazwan?

Kashmiri hosts press food on their guests, and refusing a dish or an extra helping is considered disrespectful. The gushtaba is accepted, and shared.
