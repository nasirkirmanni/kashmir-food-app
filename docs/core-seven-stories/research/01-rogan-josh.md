# Rogan josh: fact ledger

_Every claim the article makes, with its source. Source ids are in `sources.md`. "Uncited" means the statement on that page carries no footnote._

## The name

- Spellings roghan josh, roghan ghosht; "unclear which name is original" (Ayto 2012) — wp-rogan-josh.
- Persian reading: *roghen* = ghee or oil (Collingham 2006); *jōš* = to stew or braise (Delahunty 2008); so "stewed in ghee" — wp-rogan-josh.
- Kashmiri reading: *roghan* = "red" (Chapman 2009), with *gosht* "meat" (Ayto) or a word for "juice" (Wahhab 2016): "red meat" or "red juice" — wp-rogan-josh.
- "The exact etymology remains uncertain" (Ayto) — wp-rogan-josh.
- "Rogan means fat and Josh means to cook on high heat" — onmanorama.
- Collingham: the Persian name "implies a stew of meat cooked in butter"; "some food historians" link it to red, roghan meaning red in Kashmiri — dawn-2014.
- Grierson, *rōgan*, p. 828: "grease, varnish, polish; clarified butter, ghī" — grierson.
- The names rogan josh, yakhaen, ab gosht, riste and goshtabh "stem from Persian sources" (cites Rather et al. 2016) — wp-kashmiri-cuisine.
- The finished dish is judged by its oil: simmer "until the meat is tender and the gravy is red, thick, and oily" — kaul-kilam recipe 40; "mutton slow cooked in ghee and its own juices" — outlook-traveller-2017 glossary.

## Where it came from

- Persian cuisine's "dish of meat fried in hot butter", brought to North India by the Mughals in the 16th century — wp-rogan-josh (sentence uncited).
- Collingham: the summer heat of the plains led the Mughals to Kashmir, where they adapted Kashmiri herbs for colour and flavour; the dish became a staple and a main dish of the wazwan — wp-rogan-josh.
- Collingham quoted: "Rogan Josh originated in Persia"; the Mughals "encouraged a blossoming of Kashmiri cuisine"; the dish "was perfected" in Kashmir; the Mughals cultivated saffron and asafoetida (Ain-e-Akbari) — dawn-2014.
- Vir Sanghvi: "came to India with the Mughals (we think) perhaps from Persia or some other part of the Middle East"; "cooked at Mughal courts everywhere" but "really found favour only in Kashmir"; "It is the Kashmiris who created the version we know today" — vir-sanghvi.
- Kashmir came under Mughal rule in 1586 — the site's history guide (Wikipedia, Mughal conquest of Kashmir).
- Shams-ud-din Ahmad, *Shah-i-Hamdan aur Kashmir*: Persian chefs taught Kashmiris mutton dishes including kabab, roganjosh, korma, yakhni, pulao, aabgosh, kofta and rista; in the context of the saints from Iran and Central Asia (the article names the 14th-century Sayeed Ameer Kabir Hamdani) — kashmir-walla (the book is summarised by the article, not read).
- Prof Mohammad Ashraf Wani: "Islam was not introduced in Kashmir just as a religion but more like a culture"; the saints brought food, clothing and traditions, "That is when many dishes of wazwan were introduced"; "we won't find dishes with equal taste even in Iran and Central Asia" — kashmir-walla.
- Prof Fayaz: no wazwan-like cuisine in the *Nilmat Puran* or *Rajatarangini*; experts who searched Central Asia "could not find these there" — kashmir-life-2011.
- The *Tuhfat-ul-Ahbab*'s 16th-century feasts had kabab, pulao and yakhni (rogan josh not named in the summary) — kashmir-life-2021.
- No document records when or how Wazwan began — jk-arts-2024; Timur never entered Kashmir — the site's history guide.
- "Traditional knowledge is poorly documented" — rather-2016 abstract.

## Colour

- Kashmiri chilli: 1,000 to 2,000 SHU; two branded powders tested at 48,000 and 60,000 SHU (CERC) — wp-kashmiri-chilli (already on the site).
- The red can come from Kashmiri red chilli, *lal mirch* (Majumdar) — wp-rogan-josh. Whole dried chillies de-seeded, soaked and ground to a paste (uncited) — wp-rogan-josh.
- Grierson, *marʦ*, p. 594: red chilli; *-wã̄gan-āra* "a chain or string of red chillis, dried and kept for winter use" — grierson (already on the site).
- Alkanet, *Alkanna tinctoria*, *ratan jot*, dried flowers or root (Wahhab) — wp-rogan-josh; its dye dissolves in oil, not water — wp-alkanet; Pandit cuisine uses ratanjot for colour in rogan josh (uncited) — wp-kashmiri-cuisine.
- Praan and petals of *maval*, the cockscomb flower, for colour and a supposed "cooling" effect (Panjabi) — wp-rogan-josh.
- Mawal boiled to make a red colouring used mostly in Wazwan (uncited) — wp-kashmiri-cuisine; mawal gives the deep red (Azmat Ali Mir) — asia-media-centre; "the dried flower of the cockscomb plant (maval)" in the Muslim version (Collingham) — dawn-2014; "mawal flower essence" — onmanorama; "cockscomb flower extracts" plus red chillies and saffron ground in water for a dark red — kashmir-walla; mawal flower stock — chef-jolly.
- Saffron extract colours rogan josh and rista (Chef Jolly) — wp-kashmiri-cuisine.
- Colour sometimes from a packet: Bashir Ahmad Khan "not averse to using food grade colors" if other ingredients are unadulterated — kashmir-life-2011.
- Tomato: wrongly used when mawal and ratan jot are unavailable; "No authentic Kashmir-cooked version uses tomatoes" (cites a 2018 blog) — wp-kashmiri-cuisine; tomato reached Indian cooking after Europeans introduced it — wp-tomato (already on the site).

## The two versions

- Pandits: asafoetida and curd; originally no onion or garlic, "now many of them have acquired a taste for them"; young goat preferred; cuts from legs, neck, breast, ribs and shoulders; curd in almost every dish — jktdc.
- "Pandit cuisine uses dried ginger and asafoetida ... generously, while Muslim cookery uses onions and garlic"; shallot with garlic by Muslims; roganjosh in Pandit cookery "a spicy gravy with lamb chunks" — daily-excelsior-2023.
- Pandits use saunth, aniseed, hing and dry spices (Vir); garam masala sometimes at the end (Haksar); colour from Kashmiri chillies and "the browning of the yoghurt"; Muslims use onion and garlic and extract colour from mawal; the Muslim version has ghee or animal fat (Mehrotra's notes); Bamzai: "completely different, not at all alike"; Vir: "still recognisably the same dish" — vir-sanghvi.
- Brahmans avoid onions and garlic and use fennel seeds and asafoetida; the Muslim version uses "lots of garlic and onion" and cockscomb (Collingham); frying asafoetida "released a garlicky flavour" — dawn-2014.
- Brahmin versions "preclude the use of onions, ginger and garlic" (Majumdar) — wp-rogan-josh.
- A Pandit writer: a good rogan josh is cooked with little water and plenty of mustard oil — outlook-pandit (already on the site).
- Kaul Kilam's Neni Rogan Josh: mustard oil, curd, cloves, salt, sugar, red chilli powder, turmeric, dry ginger, aniseed powder, cumin, garam masala, a teaspoon of ghee, a pinch of asafoetida; no onion, garlic or ratan jot; curd-coated meat fried in mustard oil until golden brown; chilli, turmeric and sugar with half a cup of water until brownish-red; then about two cups of water with dry ginger and aniseed; simmered until the gravy is "red, thick, and oily"; ghee and garam masala for the last two or three minutes — kaul-kilam recipe 40.
- The Wazwan version: praan with garlic (sahapedia); praan "the life of Wazwan", ghee for an authentic Wazwan, mustard oil at home (Azmat Ali Mir) — asia-media-centre; "Praan paste, mawal flower essence, curd, and Kashmiri mirchi" — onmanorama; "flower essence, curd, and Kashmiri chilly" — gulf-news-2022; some versions include onions or yoghurt (Panjabi) — wp-rogan-josh.
- Pandits prefer lamb or sheep (Roy 2016) — wp-kashmiri-cuisine; JKTDC says young goat. Disagreement noted.

## The cut

- 70 to 80 g pieces from the lower ribs, the lower back (*bukhtor*), the chest and the neck — sahapedia.
- Medium chunks from the lower back, slow-cooked; about twenty pieces per utensil (Mohammad Shafi Khan) — kashmir-walla.
- "For Rogan josh neck and shoulders of the meat are preferable" (Bashir Ahmad Choncha) — deccan-chronicle.
- Shoulder meat — onmanorama; shoulder cuts — gulf-news-2022; shoulder, saddle and ribs — chef-jolly.
- Rogan josh and korma from the bony portions of the lamb — kashmir-life-2011.
- "more prosperous families used better cuts of meat", "the whole machhli, the most tender part of the shoulder" (Haksar); Razdan "never had" the lamb-shank version growing up; "perhaps the cut you use is only a function of what you can afford"; fancy restaurants' "Kareli Rogan Josh" with shank — vir-sanghvi.
- Meat cooked within one hour of butchering — onmanorama; slaughtered only hours before cooking — jk-arts-2024.
- Gravy of garlic, ginger, red chilli powder, clove, cardamom, cinnamon and praan paste; served hot with rice — sahapedia.
- Garlic, ginger, clove, bay leaves, cardamom, cinnamon (Panjabi); finished with *dampokhtak* slow cooking (Singh 1973) — wp-rogan-josh.

## How the wazas cook

- Cooking starts around 3 am — onmanorama; jk-arts-2024. Cooking from dawn for about six hours, after a night of chopping and pounding over 200 kg — kashmir-walla. Days of 18 to 20 hours in the season — sahapedia.
- The *vur*, an open kitchen with firewood "logs and birch branches"; degs "under an autumn moon" — gulf-news-2022. Temporary kitchens at the host's home; *daigs*; fruit-tree wood, apple, walnut or plum — sahapedia. Nickel-plated copper *daegs* — outlook-diplomacy; nickel-plated copper on fruit-tree logs — onmanorama; on dried wood — jk-arts-2024. *Degchis* over a wood fire — reuters-2026. Brass vats on a trench fire, heat regulated by moving them — outlook-traveller-2017.
- Heat "on two sides, top and bottom"; "best results ... by slow heat using charcoal" — daily-excelsior-2023. Chef Nazir Ahmed Aram: pots need the right copper content — wp-kashmiri-cuisine (fn 138).
- The vasta waza supervises 15 to 20 degs; apprentices chop, portion and pound — asia-media-centre. Wazas speak a code language — jk-arts-2024.
- "In wazwan hurry does not achieve the best curry" (Bashir Ahmad Khan) — kashmir-life-2011.
- Served "exactly four pieces of a course" to four spots on the trami — gulf-news-2022.
- Mushk budji, the rice prized for Wazwan (Azmat Ali Mir) — asia-media-centre; GI tag February 2022 — wp-kashmiri-cuisine.

## Its place in the feast

- One of the seven "a must" — wp-wazwan; "among the seven permanent dishes" — dawn-2014; among the seven "indispensable" — roli listing; among the seven "standard dishes that are must" — daily-excelsior-2023.
- Order: after methi and rista — outlook-diplomacy; after rista, before daniphol and aab gosht — asia-media-centre; in the mid-meal group with daniwal korma, haakh, marchwagun korma and doon chetin — gulf-news-2022; rista first (Khan) — kashmir-walla.
- "the order is set in stone" (Azmat Ali Mir) — asia-media-centre; "no set rules" on which dishes are served — outlook-diplomacy.
- Radish chutney between two heavy meat dishes — jk-arts-2024; onmanorama.
- "easily 2 kg of mutton and chicken are served per person" — onmanorama (IANS).
- Gushtaba last — many; see sources.md.

## Neighbours and the dish abroad

- Marchwangan korma: "smaller chunks of meat than used in roganjosh and cooked in a thick chilli gravy" — kashmir-walla; "three-quarters of the chilly used in the entire wazwan" — onmanorama; "fiery hot" — outlook-diplomacy; gulf-news-2022.
- Yakhni without turmeric or chilli powder — wp-list. Daniwal korma "white gravy flavoured with coriander" — outlook-traveller-2017.
- British Indian restaurants: red with tomatoes and red peppers (Dillon, BBC); Western versions add tomatoes; pour-over sauces tomato-based (uncited); Singh 1973: tomatoes not traditional; Holkar 1975 "meat and tomatoes"; Bhangal 2013 Punjabi tomato version; Jaffrey: "4 to 16 times as much paprika as cayenne"; Kapoor: white and black cardamom, anise and bay; Owen 1994: beef brisket; Collingham: foreign visitors including Americans had their first Indian food at these restaurants — wp-rogan-josh.
- Kwality-Volga "dubious variation"; England's curry houses; "The Punjabi restaurant variation is based on the Hindu recipe" — vir-sanghvi.
- Eat it with rice, "not with a roti or a naan" (Haksar) — vir-sanghvi; bread in Kashmir is eaten with tea — wp-kashmiri-cuisine (already on the site).
