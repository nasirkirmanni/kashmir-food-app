— THE FEAST · 20 DISHES

Thirty-six courses,
one copper plate.

Meat is the language. The waza is fluent.

A traditional wazwan is cooked overnight by a head waza and his team, then carried out course by course on a shared trami. Guests sit four to a plate; the meal ends, always, with gushtaba — pounded mutton balls in a whipped yoghurt gravy — served last because it's considered the richest.

— WARMTH IN A CUP · 4 BEVERAGES

Kahwa keeps
the cold honest.

Poured from a samovar, not a kettle.

Saffron, cinnamon, cardamom, and crushed almonds, kept simmering in a copper samovar through the day. Kahwa is the everyday cup; noon chai — salted, pink, and served with bread — is the one that shows up at weddings and Friday breakfasts alike.

— THE MORNING BREAD · 6 VARIETIES

Bread that's bought,
never baked at home.

Every mohalla has its kandur.

Kashmiri bread is a bakery good, not a kitchen one — fetched fresh each morning from the neighbourhood kandur (baker), still warm, in a cloth or a bare hand. Girda for everyday chai, sheermal for something sweeter, bakarkhani for weekends.

— THE OTHER TABLE · 7 EATS

The valley's second
menu, eaten standing.

No trami here. Just a paper plate.

Away from the formality of a wazwan is a looser, faster table — fried fish by the river, monje pakodas on a rainy afternoon, nadru fritters from a cart outside school. Cheaper, quicker, and just as particular about getting it right.
